# Buy the Guns, Record the Poilus

The best way to sound Verdun is to buy **about $1,260 of professional libraries**, then spend real effort on custom foley and native-speaker voices, because no store sells the French side of the war. Five purchases cover the core. **BOOM Library's World War I Firearms** is the only library recorded from genuine WW1 weapons, with close, mid and far mics in two environments. **Pole Position's Kar98k** is the closest recorded stand-in for the Gewehr 98. **The Recordist's Vickers Gun** is the only commercial recording of a Maxim-family machine gun, recorded out to 150 m. **Pole Position's Warfare Library bundle** has real 70/105/155 mm artillery recorded at both the gun and the impact site, with incoming shell whines. **BOOM's Crowds: War & Battle** supplies charge walla, pain and death cries. What no vendor sells is just as important. There are no recordings of the Lebel, Berthier, Chauchat, Hotchkiss M1914, MG08, French 75 or German 77, no gas-attack library, no WW1 whistles or rattles, and no French or German soldier voices. Those gaps are filled by proxies (the Vickers for the MG08, the Kar98k for the Gewehr 98), a home foley session, and native-actor voice sessions in the low thousands of dollars. Licensing suits a browser game as long as two rules hold: buy perpetual, one-time licenses rather than subscriptions, and ship all paid audio inside the project's existing encrypted pack. Babylon's audio engine accepts a decrypted `ArrayBuffer` directly, so no raw sound file ever needs a public URL. The only authentic WW1 battlefield recording is a 1918 gramophone disc, so archives provide period flavour, not gameplay sound. Prices below are list prices seen on 3 October 2026 and change often, especially during Sonniss and A Sound Effect sales.

## One library records real WW1 guns, and it skips Verdun's

**BOOM Library's World War I Firearms** (released 6 June 2025) is the only professional library built around WW1 weapons. It was recorded outdoors from original period firearms: four revolvers, five pistols, seven rifles and two shotguns. Every weapon was miked at close, mid and far positions in two acoustic settings, one "hard, reflective" and one "softer" setting surrounded by vegetation. It is delivered as 24-bit/96 kHz stereo, Ambisonics and ORTF3D, and includes cocking, reloading, dry-fire, misfire and handling sounds ([BOOM](https://www.boomlibrary.com/sound-effects/world-war-i-firearms/); [Production Expert](https://www.production-expert.com/production-expert-1/boom-library-world-war-i-firearms-sound-effects-library-released)). The **Bundle has 571 files and 4,594 sounds in 15.41 GB**. Launch prices were $135 Designed, $205 Construction Kit and $259 Bundle ([Sound on Sound](https://www.soundonsound.com/news/boom-library-release-world-war-firearms)), but the product page now shows "from $139" for every edition, so check the price at checkout. Buy the Construction Kit or the Bundle. The 72-file Designed edition bakes in cinematic tails and is too small to cover hundreds of shooters.

The weapon list is the problem. On the German side it has only the **Luger P08 and Mauser C96**. It has **no Lebel, Berthier or Gewehr 98, and no machine gun of any kind** ([BOOM](https://www.boomlibrary.com/sound-effects/world-war-i-firearms/)). A search across professional vendors and marketplaces found no commercial recording of the Lebel 1886, Berthier, Chauchat, Hotchkiss M1914, St. Étienne M1907, MG08 or MG08/15, Lewis gun, Ruby pistol or Lebel M1892 revolver. The only Lebel audio found was YouTube footage linked from a Great War Forum thread ([Great War Forum](https://www.greatwarforum.org/topic/254810-sound-profiles-of-lebel-m1886-french-rifles-vs-mauser-gewehr-98/)), and the only Maxim audio was free, low-grade single files ([Freesound](https://freesound.org/s/160771/)). The gap is not limited to specialist vendors. The game-asset marketplaces have nothing either ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/sound-library/world-war-i-firearms/)).

Proxies fill most of the German gaps well. The **Kar98k** shares the Gewehr 98's Mauser 98 action and 7.92×57 round, and at RTS distance the longer-barrelled original will sound almost the same. **Pole Position's Mauser Karabiner 98 kurz** ($149, 104 files) is the deepest Kar98k recording: **24 perspectives across 44 channels**, two locations, and separate "longer and also shorter, concise gunfire tails" ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/mauser-karabiner-98-kurz-k98k-bolt-action-rifle/)). For the MG08, **The Recordist's Vickers Gun HD Pro** ($89, 41 files, 471 sounds) is a real, "authentic, not a replica" Vickers. Like the MG08, the Vickers is a recoil-operated, water-cooled Maxim derivative. It was recorded with 10 mono and 7 stereo mics "from just a few inches out to 150 meters", and "99% of the tracks are without noise reduction" ([The Recordist](https://therecordist.com/vickers-machine-gun-released/); [product page](https://therecordist.com/product/vickers-gun/)). Since the MG08 is the signature sound players will learn to fear, this $89 library is the most valuable single purchase per dollar.

The French arsenal has to be faked. The Lebel and Berthier can be covered by another full-power bolt rifle, such as BOOM WW1's Mosin-Nagant 1891 or Springfield M1903. The Lebel's tube-magazine loading has no equivalent anywhere and needs custom foley. The Hotchkiss M1914 is gas-operated and strip-fed with a slower cycle than the Maxim, so it is best built by re-sequencing Vickers bursts or Browning M1919 sources. The M1919 is cheap to get in **Superthump's Weapons of World War II** ($55 on Sonniss, $40 on A Sound Effect), which also has the BAR, MG42, Kar98, Luger and Springfield ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/weapons-world-war-ii-designed/); [A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/product-tag/mg42/)). It is also in **Fascinated Sound's Gun Locker** ($109), which has a tank-mounted M1919 ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/gun-locker-sfx-pack/)). **BOOM's World War II Firearms** includes an MG42 recorded with a Sennheiser AMBEO at 100 m ([BOOM](https://www.boomlibrary.com/sound-effects/world-war-ii-firearms/)), but the MG42's much faster cyclic rate makes it a poor Maxim substitute without heavy editing.

Several secondary libraries add variation and handling detail:

At a camera 50–300 m above the line, the far perspectives and tails do most of the work. Close layers are needed only for the selected squad and for UI feedback. That is why multi-perspective source libraries (BOOM's Construction Kits, Pole Position's 24 perspectives, the Recordist's 150 m mics) matter more than designed one-shots: distance becomes a crossfade between real recordings rather than an EQ guess.

## Artillery has to be built from modern guns and real detonations

No library records a WW1 gun firing live shells. Every artillery sound will be a stand-in. **Pole Position Production's Warfare Library 3: Heavy Bombardment** ($199, 140 files, 17.29 GB) is the strongest one. It contains real 70 mm, 105 mm and "Haubits" 155 mm artillery, recorded **at both the firing site and the impact site, plus perspectives in between**. It includes incoming projectile whines and whistles, impacts, debris and "extended reverb tails", delivered in mono, stereo, surround and B-format ambisonics ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/the-warfare-library-3-heavy-bombardment/)). Impact-site recordings with real incoming shells are the scarcest material in this whole category. Pole Position also recorded the WW1-era vehicles for Battlefield 1 ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/battlefield-1-sound/)), which makes it the closest thing to a proven WW1-game artillery vendor.

**Warfare 2** ($199, 627 files) was recorded at a real military exercise from 22 perspectives, including **4 channels inside a bunker**. That suits dugout and trench listener positions. It covers troop, vehicle and tank mines, "artillery grenade and C-4 plastic explosive blasts", and debris settling, and it ships Pro Tools and Reaper sessions with all perspectives aligned ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/the-warfare-2-library/)). The **three-volume bundle costs $399 instead of $597** (943 files, 49.2 GB) ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects-tag/howitzer/)). That is the right buy once the game moves past a single test sector.

Three more libraries complete the impact palette:

- **BOOM Cannons** (from $139): 15 black-powder replicas, including Mortar 45, 66 and 80. They were recorded on 26 channels with an AMBEO at 100 m, and include flybys and impacts on water, wood, metal, concrete, stone and sand ([BOOM](https://www.boomlibrary.com/sound-effects/cannons/); [Toolfarm](https://www.toolfarm.com/?p=102185)). Its low-velocity mortars are the natural source for the Minenwerfer's thump.
- **Krotos/Barney Oram's Explosions** ($199, 388 files at 24-bit/192 kHz): real black powder, C4, detcord, Semtex and TNT miked **from 20 m to 300 m** ([Krotos](https://www.krotosaudio.com/?p=1020473)).
- **Tonsturm**: *Indoor Explosions* was recorded in an abandoned hangar, halls and corridors, which suits Fort Douaumont and Vaux interiors ([Strong Mocha](https://strongmocha.com/vendor/tonsturm/tonsturm-new-indoor-explosions-effects-library)). It also sells *Tremor* and *The Whoosh*. Prices were not published on its homepage ([Tonsturm](https://www.tonsturm.com/)).

For the heaviest guns, **Pole Position's Bandkanon 1C** ($99–299) is a 155 mm self-propelled gun recorded from 27 perspectives ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects-tag/artillery/)). **BOOM Armageddon** (from $139) supplies rumble, collapsing terrain and thunder ([BOOM](https://www.boomlibrary.com/sound-effects/armageddon/)). Because the sources are recorded at 96–192 kHz, they can be pitched down hard to fake 210 mm and 420 mm reports without aliasing.

### Whizz-bangs and howitzers must sound different

History gives a gameplay rule. Field-gun shells (the French 75 and German 77, the "whizz-bangs") were supersonic. Near the target a soldier heard the burst, then the whizz, and only then the gun, so they gave **almost no warning**. The whistle of slower howitzer shells came from the shell's motion and its rotating driving band, not from any attached whistle ([Great War Forum](https://www.greatwarforum.org/topic/299289-did-shells-really-whistle/); [Great War Forum](https://www.greatwarforum.org/topic/53768-just-what-exactly-was-a-whizz-bang/)). So 75 and 77 mm shells should arrive as a short crack and whizz with no lead-in. The 155, 210 and 420 mm shells should get the long descending rush that lets players dive for cover. That rush can be designed from Warfare 3's incoming recordings, BOOM Cannons flybys and Tonsturm's *The Whoosh*.

Gas is the inverse cue. Gas shells came over with a quiet hiss and burst with a dull thud, and were often mistaken for duds ([Pritzker Military Museum](https://www.pritzkermilitary.org/node/781); [State Library NSW](https://transcripts.sl.nsw.gov.au/node/100156)). Players should learn to listen for **the absence of a bang**. **No gas-attack library exists.** Build the gas shell from a small black-powder or grenade-class detonation plus pressure hiss, for example from Tim Prebble's HISS and a ROAR *Pressure*, which has 2,700+ sounds from steam trains, pressure cookers and aerosols ([Sonic Field](https://sonicfield.org/making-of-hiss-and-a-roar-pressure-sfx-library-by-tim-prebble)). The alarms themselves were hand-spun ratchet rattles, gongs made from empty brass shell cases, sirens and wooden clappers ([RMR Museum](https://rmrmuseum.com/gas-alarms-in-ww1/); [Science Museum](https://collection.sciencemuseum.org.uk/objects/co154650/gas-alert-rattle-england-1917-rattle)). A football rattle and a struck brass case recorded at home will be more authentic than any stock sound, and carry no license at all. Whether French units at Verdun specifically used klaxons was not established.

### Layer shells the way Battlefield 1 did

DICE split each Battlefield 1 explosion into "shockwave, environmental report and debris raining down on the soldier". It built shrapnel from bullet impacts and flybys sequenced past the player, recorded debris by throwing dirt onto uniform pieces and a helmet, and filled the distance with one-shot "big world sounds" and "distant rumbles of thunder", with loud events ducking quiet ones ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/battlefield-1-sound/)). That maps directly onto a WebAudio layer stack:

1. Incoming: whizz for field guns, rush for howitzers.
2. Transient crack.
3. Body, chosen by distance band.
4. Sub-bass thump.
5. Debris, sized by calibre.
6. Shrapnel flybys, for close hits only.
7. Terrain tail.
8. Tinnitus and ducking on a near miss. Designed ear-ringing usually sits around 3–5 kHz ([Morphic](https://morphic.com/resources/sounds/ear-ringing-sound-effect)).

Under drumfire, hundreds of shells a minute will smear any baked-in tail, so Construction Kit and multi-perspective source material beats designed files. Cheap designed packs such as Bluezone's $23.50 *Artillery* mix in "electronic elements" ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects-tag/howitzer/)) and €5–10 Unity artillery packs fall below the quality bar.

## Ambience, foley and voices are where custom work pays

**No AAA library is dedicated to WW1 trench ambience.** Battlefield 1 did not use one either. Mari Saastamoinen Minto used her own recordings from France, "researching what birds and animals are found in that specific part of the world", and baked her own rain recordings in ([A Sound Effect](https://asoundeffect.com/battlefield-1-sound/)). Build ambience the same way: a distant war bed from the far channels of Warfare 2 and 3, the Krotos 300 m mics and BOOM Cannons' "Distant" set ([Toolfarm](https://www.toolfarm.com/?p=102185)), plus premium nature layers. Thomas Rex Beverly's libraries are the quality benchmark (*High Desert Thunderstorms* $45, *Nature Roomtones: Deserts* $80; credits include *The Last of Us*) ([A Sound Effect](https://asoundeffect.com/sounddesigner/thomas-rex-beverly)), but they were recorded in the US, so check the bird species before using them over the Meuse. Budget battlefield beds exist, such as Fusehive's *Warfare Ambiences* at $149.99 with 2h21m of soundscapes ([GameDev Market](https://gamedevmarket.net/asset/warfare-ambiences-sound-effects-library-army-combat-battlefield-weapon-war-background-environment)), but their quality is unverified. The Imperial War Museum's "Trench Warfare" compilation was not accessible for review ([IWM](https://www.iwm.org.uk/collections/item/object/80000964)).

Military foley has the same pattern: generic material is excellent, and WW1-specific kit is absent. Mud footsteps and dirt foley are plentiful ([A Sound Effect footsteps](https://asoundeffect.com/sound-category/foley/footsteps); [MuseHub](https://musehub.com/loops-and-audio/earth-dirt-and-mud-foley-sound-effects)). A "World War Soldier" set exists inside the *MOVES* gear-movement collection ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/sound-category/foley)), but its publisher and price were not confirmed. Gas-mask breathing exists only as single stock tracks ([Artlist](https://artlist.io/sfx/track/organic-tension---gas-mask-breath-exhale-inhale-slowly/56520)). The only "Thunderer" whistle on sale is a modern plastic one ([Soundstripe](https://www.soundstripe.com/library/sound-effects/32341)). Nothing was found for field-telephone magneto rings, rats, dugout room tones, French *clairon* bugle calls or Very pistols; searches surfaced only AI-generated "flare" sounds.

One home foley session would cover all of that and stay consistent in perspective. It needs:

- An Acme Thunderer and a reproduction trench whistle.
- A ratchet rattle and a brass shell case for gas alarms.
- A blank-firing flare pistol pop layered with a road-flare fizz for Very lights.
- Garden wire and bolt cutters for barbed wire.
- Wooden pallets over wet soil for duckboards.
- A shovel, sandbags and a wooden ladder.
- A canteen and webbing.
- Crawling in mud.
- A rubber mask breathing through a cloth pad, for period gas masks, which were muffled rubber and fabric rather than modern valve clicks.

At a top-down camera, many variations matter more than close-mic detail, which favours a long, cheap home session over a short, expensive studio one. The prop budget was not researched.

### Voices: buy the screams, commission the words

Non-verbal vocals are well covered. **BOOM's Crowds: War & Battle** (January 2025, **$329**, 1,251 files and 3,935 sounds) has single male and female one-shots plus outdoor group recordings on a 22-channel rig. They are processed to different group sizes, distances and intensities, across agony, fear, pain, fight, begging, death and war cries ([BOOM](https://www.boomlibrary.com/sound-effects/crowds-war-battle/)). That is the charge-walla layer for hundreds of soldiers. BOOM's *Creatures Humanoid* adds 15 male and 7 female sets of pain, death and effort sounds ([Rekkerd](https://rekkerd.org/boom-library-releases-creatures-humanoid-sound-library/)). 344 SFX's *British Soldier Voices* ($64, 1,600+ files) has language-neutral grunts and efforts under its English phrases ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/?p=25416)).

Language is the hard gap. **No French soldier voice library exists.** The German options are 46 parade-ground shouts at 16-bit/44.1 kHz ([Shockwave-Sound](https://shockwave-sound.com/sound-effects/12396/sonicarchive-military-personnel-german)) and scattered drill commands ([Soundstripe](https://www.soundstripe.com/library/sound-effects/2870)). Blackmill's WW1 games are praised specifically for native voice acting ([TechRadar](https://global.techradar.com/nl-be/reviews/isonzo-review)). Commission three or four native French and three or four native German male actors for squad orders and officer barks in period idiom, such as "En avant!", "À la baïonnette!", "Hurra!" and "Sprung auf, marsch, marsch!", with a historical-language reviewer checking the script.

Costs depend on union status:

- **SAG-AFTRA:** an off-camera session of up to three voices in four hours pays **$1,134.95**. Vocally stressful sessions (screaming, death sounds, battle cries) are capped at two hours. A tiered agreement exists for independent budgets ([SAG-AFTRA](https://sagaftra.org/industry-resources/rates-agreements/tiered-budget-independent-interactive-media-agreement)).
- **Non-union:** secondary sources cite **$200–$350+ per game session**, or $300–$600 per two-to-four-hour indie session ([GVAA](https://globalvoiceacademy.com/gvaa-rate-guide-2/); [VoxBooster](https://voxbooster.com/blog/voiceover-rates-statistics-2026/)).
- **Marketplaces:** VoiceBros, Voice123 and Voices.com supply native actors with commercial rights ([VoiceBros](https://voicebros.com/fr/voice-actors/french)).

Seven actors at non-union rates comes to roughly **$2k–$4k**, an estimate rather than a quote. In a top-down mix walla dominates, so these lines matter most for the player's own squad and its officers. AI voice placeholders were not researched for consent or union implications.

### Aircraft are solved; vehicles and horses need substitutes

Period aircraft are the best-served category. **Pole Position's Fokker Dr.I 1917** ($449, 8.09 GB) records a real Le Rhône 9J rotary on 22 synchronised channels, with 9 exterior perspectives and 5 onboard ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/sound-effects/fokker-dr-1-1917/)). The French Nieuport fighters also used Le Rhône rotaries (not verified in this research), so one library plausibly covers both sides at overhead distance. The **Fokker D.VII 1918** ($449) has a Daimler D.III inline engine, but the aircraft dates from 1918, two years late ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/product/fokker-d-vii-1918)). **BOOM Horses** (347 sounds, 9.0 GB) covers gaits on mud, dirt, gravel and stone, plus tack ([Sound Ideas](https://sound-ideas.com/Product/178/Horses-Sound-Effects)). No library covers WW1 trucks such as the Berliets of the Voie Sacrée, field kitchens, horse-drawn limbers or Verdun's church bells. WWII-era vehicles, such as Black Edge Audio's *1940 Military Gear* ([Gumroad](https://blackedgeaudio.gumroad.com/l/1940militarygear)), are the nearest substitutes.

## Game marketplaces and archives add flavour, not fidelity

The game-asset stores add almost nothing.

- **Fab:** the only WW1-tagged audio is a generic "151 World War Sound Effects" pack of unknown provenance ([Fab](https://www.fab.com/listings/468deb2f-7479-46d3-b692-eca5593690c7)). Fab audio aimed at Unreal may ship as `.uasset`, so check each listing for WAV delivery.
- **Unity Asset Store:** the strongest period option is SoundFX Studio's *WWII Weapons Sound System* ($99.99 per part). It has too few ratings to judge, and its 12 weapons include no WW1 arms beyond the Mauser C96 and Browning Auto-5 ([Unity](https://assetstore.unity.com/packages/audio/sound-fx/weapons/wwii-weapons-sound-system-part-1-326501); [SoundFX Studio](https://soundfx.studio/shop/sfx-for-games/weapons/wwii-weapons-sound-fx-library-part-i/)).
- **itch.io and GameDev Market:** only generic "war" packs. Some itch packs are MP3-only, which is a quality red flag ([itch.io](https://delusiondrive.itch.io/sounds-of-war-full-audio-effects-pack-in-mp3-format)).
- **Free "WW1" sound sites:** ElevenLabs' "World War" sounds are AI-generated ([ElevenLabs](https://elevenlabs.io/sound-effects/world-war)), and Pixabay's thousands of WW1 results are user uploads of unknown origin ([Pixabay](https://pixabay.com/sound-effects/world-war-1-trench-warfare-battlefield-sounds-6744/)).

**A Sound Effect and Sonniss are the real stores for this project.** They carry BOOM, Watson Wu, Pole Position and GCRadix under perpetual royalty-free licenses. Sonniss also gives away a royalty-free, no-attribution **GDC Game Audio Bundle** every year. The 2026 bundle is 7.47 GB+ ([rekkerd](https://rekkerd.org/sonniss-releases-gdc-2026-game-audio-bundle/)), though which explosion packs it contains was not checked. The Dutch studio Game Audio Squad, which did sound for Verdun, Tannenberg and Isonzo (Isonzo won Dutch Game Awards Best Audio 2023), sells no library ([Game Audio Squad](https://www.gameaudiosquad.com/projects/isonzo)). Its level of quality is reachable only by commission.

### Only one authentic WW1 battle recording survives

Archives yield one genuine battlefield recording: **Will Gaisberg's "Gas Shell Bombardment" (HMV 09308), recorded near Lille on 9 October 1918** and marketed as "not an imitation" ([Sound of the Hound](https://soundofthehound.com/2018/11/09/actual-record-taken-on-the-front-line-france-near-lille-november-1918/)). Even that is now thought to be a "double- or triple-layered sonic artifact" ([Jagged Lines](https://jaggedlines.substack.com/p/what-did-world-war-i-sound-like-cfa0a455844c)). As a published pre-1923 recording it is public domain in the US ([US Copyright Office](https://www.copyright.gov/music-modernization/pre1972-soundrecordings/)). In the UK a straight transfer creates no new copyright ([CDPA s.5A(2)](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/5A)). The safe route is to transfer a purchased original 78 yourself rather than lift a remaster from an archive website.

The famous IWM "Armistice" audio is **not a recording**. It is a 2018 re-creation by Coda to Coda, built from a sound-ranging film, and therefore modern copyright ([IWM](https://www.iwm.org.uk/history/sound-ranging-explained)). The other archives are all restricted:

- **IWM** licenses its 33,000 recordings for a fee, and its licensing page names film, radio and TV, not games ([IWM](https://www.iwm.org.uk/commercial/collections-sales-licensing/sound-licensing)).
- **Gallica/BnF** charges for commercial reuse, and bars commercial use of sound supplied by Sony, Universal and Warner France ([Gallica](https://gallica.bnf.fr/edit/und/conditions-dutilisation-des-contenus-de-gallica)).
- **The Bundesarchiv** holds a few early-war recordings, mostly speeches ([Bundesarchiv](https://www.bundesarchiv.de/im-archiv-recherchieren/archivgut-recherchieren/nach-medienart/toene/)).
- **The free BBC archive** is personal, educational and research use only ([Sonicstate](https://www.sonicstate.com/news/2025/09/17/33-thousand-bbc-sound-effects/)).

Acoustic-era discs have roughly telephone bandwidth plus surface noise. Use them for a diegetic gramophone, the menu or a museum mode, never for gameplay.

## One-time licenses plus the encrypted pack clear a browser game

Every vendor reviewed allows its sounds in a commercial video game. The browser-specific risk is that every sound reaches the player's machine. Vendors split on how much protection they demand ([licensing notes](notes/audio/licensing_archives_delivery.md)):

- **Explicit protection duty:** Sound Ideas requires sounds "embedded in the code of your game and ... not available for an end-user to access or download" ([Sound Ideas FAQ](https://sound-ideas.com/pages/faq)). Soundsnap, Envato, GameDev Market, Fab and Unity all forbid letting users extract files.
- **No stated duty:** Sonniss, A Sound Effect, Splice and BOOM require only that sounds be "incorporated" or "synchronized" in the product ([Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/license); [A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/license-agreement/)).

The project's existing encrypted, worker-decrypted pack, plus a game EULA forbidding extraction, is the most defensible reading of every clause found. Apply it to all paid audio, so per-vendor tracking is unnecessary. Opus and AAC encoding are open formats and give no protection on their own. CC0 audio can ship raw. CC-BY audio must ship raw, because CC BY 4.0 forbids technological restrictions on it ([CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/legalcode)). Freesound's CC BY-NC sounds are unusable.

Three smaller clauses matter for this project:

- **Seats.** BOOM's single-user license is for "one individual only", and Sonniss requires the licensee to be the "sole editor" ([BOOM EULA](https://www.boomlibrary.com/?p=480427); [Sonniss](https://sonniss.com/license)). A contract sound designer who builds layers from the developer's purchased libraries needs a multi-user license. A designer who owns the libraries already does not.
- **Convolution reverb.** BOOM bans embedding content "where it functions as a real-time audio tool ... (e.g., Impulse Responses used in convolution reverbs)" ([BOOM EULA](https://www.boomlibrary.com/?p=480427)). Any WebAudio `ConvolverNode` impulse response should be self-recorded or CC0, not cut from a purchased tail.
- **Map editor.** A Sound Effect bans "media authoring tools" ([A Sound Effect](https://www.asoundeffect.com/license-agreement/)). A future map editor should reference sounds by ID and never export them.

Every vendor bans AI training, which matches the project rule never to upload purchased assets to AI services. Soundly goes further and bans uploading into "any third party AI tool", which covers AI cleanup services too. BOOM's product page also says its products "are only available for commercial customers" ([BOOM](https://www.boomlibrary.com/sound-effects/world-war-i-firearms/)), so check how it treats a sole trader at checkout. The BOOM EULA quotes came through a summarizing fetch and should be re-read on the live page.

### Delivery fits the existing pipeline

Babylon 9's `CreateSoundBufferAsync` accepts an `ArrayBuffer`. A worker-decrypted buffer can therefore become a shared buffer for many `StaticSound`s without any raw URL. Streaming sounds need a URL, so long paid ambiences should use Blob URLs from decrypted chunks or be decoded as static loops ([Babylon typings](https://app.unpkg.com/@babylonjs/core@9.18.0/files/AudioV2/abstractAudio/audioEngineV2.js)). Ship Opus. Chrome, Edge and Firefox play it in Ogg or WebM. Safari plays it in WebM from 17.5, and in Ogg only from 18.4 on macOS 15.4 ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/opus); [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/documentation/safari-release-notes/safari-18_4-release-notes)). That makes **Opus-in-WebM plus an AAC fallback** the safe pack format. Whether Safari's `decodeAudioData` handles WebM Opus is unverified.

Decoded memory, not download size, is the real constraint. Stereo audio at 48 kHz costs 384 KB per second as float32 ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioBuffer)), so keep spatialized one-shots mono. Set `maxInstances` per sound, since the default is unlimited. Use HRTF only for a few nearby sources, and run a priority-and-culling pass for barrages of hundreds of events.

## Conclusion

The sound market has the same shape as the 3D market: the generic battlefield is cheap and excellent, and the French soldier does not exist. The sound gap differs in what can be faked. A Lebel heard from 200 m away is a full-power bolt rifle with a tail, and a Mosin will pass. A Hotchkiss's stutter is distinctive but can be built from the Vickers. A French command shouted in an American accent, or a German one in French, cannot be faked at any distance. The first commission dollar therefore belongs to native voices for the player's own squad, not to weapon recording. A Hotchkiss and MG08 session comes second, once the prototype shows that players are learning to read machine guns by ear.

The cheapest high-impact work requires no purchase at all. The whizz-bang's silent arrival, the howitzer's warning rush and the gas shell's missing bang are rules in the simulation, not assets, and they carry more of Verdun's character than any library. Three uncertainties remain. Proxy MG08 and Hotchkiss sounds have not been compared against period reference audio. Babylon's voice limits under a barrage of hundreds of events have not been benchmarked. No vendor has defined "extraction" for browser audio. The prototype answers the first two. The third needs vendor confirmations in writing, starting with BOOM, and a lawyer before commercial launch.
