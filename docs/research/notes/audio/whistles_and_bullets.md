# Paid sound sources for trench whistles and close bullets (cracks, whizzes, ricochets, impacts, body hits)

Research date: 2026-10-04. Scope: two gaps not covered by what we already own (BOOM World War I Firearms, Pole Position
Warfare Library 1–3, Super Thump Weapons of WWII Designed, Sonniss GDC samplers). The bar is real recordings by
professional recordists at 24-bit, 48 kHz or higher; designed or synthesized stand-ins and AI-generated sound are out.
Prices are list prices seen on vendor pages on the research date (A Sound Effect, Sonniss and BOOM run frequent sales).
Licence summaries reuse [licensing, archives and delivery](licensing_archives_delivery.md) where that note already
covers a vendor. Several store pages (A Sound Effect, Soundsnap, BOOM) only render in a browser; those were read in the
built-in browser. Audiokinetic's Strata pages sit behind a CAPTCHA, so Strata facts come from search snippets. No demo
was listened to; quality judgements are from vendor descriptions, file specs and track lists only.

## Whistles

### Takeaway
No commercial library records an Acme Thunderer, an Acme Metropolitan or a WW1 trench whistle by name. The best
paid stand-in is Gee Collection's **Wild Whistles** ($25, 24-bit/96 kHz), which has about 45 files of metal "Cop",
"Officer", "Referee" and "Thin" whistles with long, short and multiple blasts. The models aren't named. A free CC-BY
recording of a real Metropolitan-style police whistle (klankbeeld, 24-bit/48 kHz) is good enough to prototype with.
For the shipped sound, buy the reproduction whistles (about £40 for three) and record them ourselves: that is the only
way to get period-exact whistles.

One historical correction matters for what we record. The whistle *issued* to British infantry officers and NCOs
was the cylinder-shaped **Acme/Hudson "Metropolitan"**, which has no pea and gives a steady shrill tone. The
**Thunderer**, the first pea whistle, gives a trill, and it also saw wide WW1 service. A trench whistle at zero
hour can be either. We should record both.

### Cited Findings

**What the period whistles were**
- "The Metropolitan 15 Broad Arrow whistle was the original 'Trench' whistle issued to Infantry Officers & NCO's".
  The Metropolitan is "a cylinder-shaped whistle without a pea". The Thunderer, the first pea whistle, "had wide
  military use as a general WW1 service whistle". — search summary of [Acme Metropolitan 15 Broad Arrow 1914](https://www.acmewhistles.co.uk/metropolitan-broadarrow-15-1914), [War Whistles: WW1 history](https://www.warwhistles.com/ww1-whistles-history.html), [J Hudson & Co (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_Hudson_%26_Co)
- War Whistles: the cylinder Metropolitan "became the standard issue in the armed forces". — [War Whistles](https://www.warwhistles.com/ww1-whistles-history.html)
- Acme's own blog describes WW1 whistles as nickel-plated brass with a cork pea and a trill "between roughly 3 kHz and
  4 kHz". This conflicts with the pea-less Metropolitan above. The blog reads like marketing copy, so treat it as
  weak. — [Acme: Loud and clear](https://www.acmewhistles.co.uk/stories/loud-and-clear-the-ww1-whistles-function)
- WW1 makers included J. Hudson & Co. (Acme), De Courcy and J. Stokes. — [Acme: story of trench whistles](https://www.acmewhistles.co.uk/stories/the-story-of-trench-whistles)
- National WWI Museum: cylinder whistles were standard trench whistles across armies. Pea ("sports") whistles were
  popular with British, Commonwealth and US troops. German, Austro-Hungarian and Ottoman makers are poorly documented
  in English, but portraits show the same cylinder and sports shapes. — [The World War (museum): Whistles](https://www.theworldwar.org/learn/about-wwi/whistles); [War History Online](https://www.warhistoryonline.com/instant-articles/whistles-used-in-the-trenches.html) (search summary)
- German: the well-documented German *Signalpfeife* (black Bakelite, single chamber, wooden ball, "harsh and
  grating") is **WW2**, not WW1. Bakelite production began in the 1920s–30s. — [Hand Grenade Division: signal whistle](https://handgrenadedivision.com/equipment/whistle.htm); [Whistle Museum](https://www.whistlemuseum.com/category/uncategorized/page/4/) (search summary)
- French: no French WW1 officer-whistle maker or pattern was found. The *sifflet à roulette* (pea/ball whistle) is the
  generic French type. — [lalanguefrancaise.com](https://www.lalanguefrancaise.com/dictionnaire/definition/sifflet-a-roulette) (search summary)

### Inferences
- Nothing on the market is a close, clean, named Acme Thunderer or Metropolitan at 24/96. Wild Whistles' "Officer" and
  "Cop" sets are the closest paid material and cost $12.50–25. Buy them as an interim placeholder and a fallback, not
  as the final asset.
- **Record our own.** Whistles: Metropolitan 15 replica £19.99 + Thunderer 58.5 £7.49 + Thunderer 58 £11.04 ≈ £39
  plus postage. Add one or two original period whistles from a militaria dealer for the German and French
  officers. Those prices weren't researched; guess £30–100 each. Total: about **£40–250**.
- Session plan: an outdoor field on a still day, or a dead room. Use a 32-bit-float or well-padded recorder: pea
  whistles are very loud at close range. Mic at about 1 m and about 5 m, 24-bit/96 kHz. Record long blasts, single
  short blasts, the double and triple-short signal patterns, trills by breath pulsing, and weak, breathless blows
  from tired men. Several blowers give natural variety. Distance and the chorus of whistles along the line belong in
  the engine, not in the recording.
- If we have no recorder, the cost is equipment (not researched here) or half a day of a local recordist (UK
  £350–500/day, from [licensing note](licensing_archives_delivery.md)). Even then it is cheaper than any library, and
  the recordings are ours outright.
- German side: use a Thunderer-type pea whistle or a brass cylinder whistle. The Bakelite *Signalpfeife* is WW2 and
  should be avoided. French side: a brass *sifflet à roulette* (pea whistle).

### Gaps
- Sound Ideas, Hollywood Edge, Soundly and BOOM catalogues weren't searchable for whistles. BOOM has no whistle
  library on its site; Sound Ideas and Hollywood Edge track lists weren't reachable.
- No primary source names a French or German WW1 officer-whistle pattern or maker.
- The Acme product page's pea claim for the Metropolitan 15 replica conflicts with every other source.

## Bullets near the listener

### Takeaway
Real live-fire, downrange recordings of bullets passing the listener exist from a few vendors only: Pole Position (we
own the Warfare Library), The Recordist (Bullets HD Pro 1–3) and BOOM Library (in *Silencers* and in BOOM ONE).
Everything else found is designed, synthesized, foley, or of unknown origin. For a WW1 game the key fact is that
every rifle and machine gun at Verdun fired supersonic ball ammunition. A near miss should be a sharp crack, not a
whizz. The whizz/whine belongs to ricochets, spent or tumbling rounds, pistol rounds and shrapnel balls. Buy
**The Recordist Bullets 3** for impacts and ricochets on many surfaces with supersonic ammunition. Buy the **BOOM
Silencers Construction Kit** for its labelled supersonic cracks (7.62 NATO, .308, .338) and its distance-logged
ricochets. Body hits are always foley: SoundMorph/Epic Stock Media **Gore** (source + designed) is the respected
choice. Real WW1-ish cracks are already in our Pole Position Warfare Library (7.62 mm MG fire from CV90s).

### Inferences
- **Ballistics for authenticity** (general reference figures, not re-verified this pass): .303 Mk VII about 2,440
  fps, 8 mm Lebel Balle D about 2,300 fps, 7.92 mm S-Patrone about 2,900 fps. All are well over the speed of sound
  (about 1,125 fps), so near misses from rifles and MGs should be a crack, followed later by the distant report.
  Subsonic whizz and whine recordings (most of Bullets HD Pro, the Silencers whiz-bys) are still authentic for
  ricochets, which lose speed and tumble. They also fit long-range spent rounds, pistols (P08 and Ruby 9 mm, .455
  Webley) and shrapnel balls. Map them to those events, not to direct rifle near-misses.
- Suppressors don't matter for this material: a suppressor removes the muzzle report, not the ballistic crack, and the
  impacts and ricochets are the same.
- The 7.62 NATO/.308 cracks in Silencers and the 7.62 MG cracks in our Warfare Library are the closest match in
  calibre and velocity to .303, 8 mm Lebel and 7.92 mm. 5.56 and 7.62×39 cracks are thinner and lighter. Use them only
  for variety.
- Steel helmets, wire and sandbags: no library names these surfaces. Build helmet hits from "metal" impacts (Bullets 3)
  plus a short real ricochet tail (Silencers). Build sandbags from dirt, sand and cloth impacts (Bullets 3 + Close
  Combat cloth). For a whine off wire, use the "Light" and "Rising" ricochets.
- Body hits: make a dry layer stack. Use a short supersonic crack (if the shot is close), a meat or wet-cloth impact
  from Gore's *source* folder or 344 Audio, and a cloth/body thump from Close Combat. Keep it dry and short; the
  engine adds distance. Avoid PMSFX's flesh hits (partly synthesized) and the designed "Bloodlust/finisher" style.

### Gaps
- No demos were auditioned. Wild Whistles, Bullets 3 and Gore should be previewed before buying.
- Bullets 3's Stage 2 status wasn't confirmed; buyers of Stage 1 were promised Stage 2 free.
- The Recordist doesn't publish per-file calibre lists on the product page. Check the sound-list PDF to see how much
  of Bullets 3 is supersonic.
- Strata Bullets' exact contents (Audiokinetic page behind a CAPTCHA) and BOOM ONE's per-file live-fire impact list
  were not reviewed.
- No library records ricochets off barbed wire, impacts on steel helmets, or hits on sandbags as named categories.
