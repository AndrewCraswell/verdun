# Level design

The prototype battlefield is the **Fleury sector**, generated by `generateFleurySector` in
[packages/map/src/fleury.ts](../packages/map/src/fleury.ts) and dressed with Synty art from the level kit. This doc
covers how the level is laid out, how the trench kit fits the simulation's terrain, and how the art reaches the game.

## The Fleury sector

600 m × 400 m of the real ground at Fleury-devant-Douaumont, north-east of Verdun, fought over through the summer
of 1916. The terrain is surveyed: IGN's LiDAR HD bare-earth model, which a century on still shows the shell holes of the
battle. The village sits on its real site, on the high ground in the east, with the ground falling some 48 m to the
west. The trench lines, the ravine and the sunken road are laid out for play on top of it.

Beyond the sector's edges the real ground goes on to the horizon (see [the world beyond](#the-world-beyond)), so the
battlefield sits in a landscape rather than on a table. [The level audit](level-audit.md) records how the sector
measured against a AAA bar and the overhaul that followed.

### Real ground

`node scripts/fetch-elevation.mjs` downloads the sector's window of IGN's LiDAR HD terrain model (MNT, 0.5 m, the
simulation's own resolution) from the Géoplateforme and writes
[packages/map/data/fleury-elevation.bin](../packages/map/data/fleury-elevation.bin): 1201 × 801 samples in centimetres
above a base height, about 1.9 MB (format in [elevation.ts](../packages/map/src/elevation.ts)). The window's south-west
corner is Lambert-93 876773 E, 6902290 N. The data is © IGN under Licence Ouverte 2.0: it ships raw, with the credit
"IGN – LiDAR HD" (see the [register](assets/register.md)).

`generateFleurySector({ elevation })` builds the sector on it: surveyed heights replace the generated rises and plateau,
the ravine and sunken road are cut into them, and about 1,500 fresh shell holes go on top of the old crater field
instead of 2,600. Mud, chalk and water follow the survey's own spread of heights. Without `elevation` the generator
still makes its synthetic ground, which the tests use.

| Feature                   | Where                         | Why it's there                                                                   |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| French lines              | South, front line at z ≈ 126  | The player's side, on the lower ground                                           |
| German lines              | North, front line at z ≈ 270  | Holding the rise toward the ridge                                                |
| Bois de la Caillette      | West, French side             | Shattered wood the French front runs through: cover, broken sightlines           |
| Ravin de la Dame          | North-west into no man's land | Dead ground for a flanking attack; only a concrete post at its head sees into it |
| Fleury                    | East, on a plateau            | Ruins where the lines close to 80 m; a French salient pushes into it             |
| Sunken road, mine craters | Middle of no man's land       | Cover for an attack across the widest stretch (about 140 m)                      |
| Chalk roads               | Rear areas and through Fleury | The supply roads, bridged where they cross trenches                              |
| Batteries                 | Behind both reserve lines     | Field guns in sandbagged pits                                                    |

Each side has three crenellated fire trenches (front, support, reserve), zigzag communication trenches between them,
saps pushed out to listening posts with machine guns and knife rests at their mouths, more machine guns on the
front-line parapet, supply dumps, and belts of wire: two French belts at about 22 m and 33 m in front of the line, three
German belts at 18, 30 and 42 m. Belts have staggered patrol gaps and gaps at the sap heads, closed as every attack
found them, at stand-to: knife rests dragged side by side across each gap of a post or two, near enough together and
overlapping the belts' ends so the simulation's wire leaves no way through (`closeGaps`). Only the sap trench runs on
through its gap, and where the lines close up the belts break off for tens of metres, open. No man's land is shelled
into churned ground with some 2,600 craters, the deeper ones holding water; craters on the ridge throw up rims of chalk,
and shelling tapers off behind the lines.

**Objectives**, the ground worth fighting for: Fleury, the Fleury crossroads, the mine craters, the Ravin de la Dame and
the ravine head. They're data for the capture rules; holding them is up to the simulation.

### The village

Fleury stands where the survey still shows it. A century of forest hasn't hidden its streets: the main street is a
smooth band running down the plateau from the German line in the north-west to the French line in the south-east, met by
a cross street near the French wire, with a lane out to the sunken road. The village is laid out on them
([fleury.ts](../packages/map/src/fleury.ts), `placeVillage`): a house on each 11 m plot either side, gable to the
street, with garden walls between the plots; a plot a big shell hole has taken holds only a heap of rubble, and a few
were yards to begin with. Rubble lies heaped against the ruins' walls. The landmark, seen from anywhere in the sector,
is the great barn of the farm on the main street with its chimney stack still standing, on the plot the shells reached
least. The village is all in no man's land, as it was in July 1916; the crossroads is an objective. No house stands
within 14 m of the map's edge.

Fleury's church stands as a ruin behind the German front on the village's north side, east of the main road
([scenery-dressing.ts](../apps/client/src/level/scenery-dressing.ts), `CHURCH`). It's built up from the War Pack's
modular walls, 2.5 m long and a storey high: a nave 10 m square broken to one storey or two, its windows blown in and
its roof fallen into it in slabs, the west tower standing three storeys to its shattered belfry over the door, and the
apse's half circle to the east. It's the landmark's second variant. It stands behind the German line rather than in no
man's land, where a building that size would hand an attack a covered jump-off; there it gives the defence depth.

### The orchard

Behind the French support line, east of the main road, stand three rows of fruit trees 6.5 m apart (`ORCHARD` in
fleury.ts): apple, pear, plum and cherry from the Farm Pack, stripped bare by the shelling (build-level-kit.py cuts
their leaves away), with a gap or a stump where a shell found one. A fruit tree is a scenery kind of its own: 2.8 m high
and thin, it hides less than a dead forest tree's 8 m trunk. Like the church, it dresses ground behind a line rather
than giving an attack cover.

The generator places the railway, the church and the orchard after everything else, the orchard on random draws of its
own, so adding them moved nothing else in the sector, and balance runs before and after compare like with like.

### The woods and no man's land

The art for the map's trees, stumps and logs ([scenery-dressing.ts](../apps/client/src/level/scenery-dressing.ts))
follows how hard the shelling hit the ground, measured by the distance to the nearest fire trench: within a few dozen
metres of the lines most trees are trunks stripped to poles, their branches shot away, leaning up to 16 degrees where
the blasts left them; far behind the lines most keep a crown of bare branches and lean a little. A log is a limb or a
length of trunk brought down, or a whole trunk felled with its shattered top. Deadwood (fallen limbs, splinters and
twigs) lies round the trees, stumps and logs, thickest near the lines, and never in a trench or a pool.

No man's land is strewn with the litter of battle: helmets of both sides, rifles, planks and sheets of iron, burst
sandbags, packs, canteens, duds and now and then a stretcher, on the churned ground only, thickest a few dozen metres
out from the trenches, where the attacks broke on the wire. The small kit is drawn half as large again so it reads from
the game's camera. None of it blocks sight or movement.

### The railway

Behind the French lines runs a 60 cm Decauville field railway, the light lines both armies laid by the thousand
kilometres to carry shells and stores up to the front ([railway.ts](../apps/client/src/level/railway.ts)). It comes in
from the sector's south edge, up from the rear toward Fort Souville, crosses the plateau behind the reserve line, passes
a dump by the main road, crosses the road, and comes down off the plateau to a railhead and its dump, where the loads
went on up the communication trenches on men's backs. It keeps to the plateau because the rest of the French rear falls
a tenth of its length toward the Meuse, far steeper than the little engines, horses and men who hauled the wagons could
manage, and the reserve line and the lateral road leave it no room further north.

The track is laid as the real thing was: prefabricated panels 2.5 m long, two rails riveted to four sleepers, bolted end
to end along the line's course. A drawn line's corners are eased into curves of about 25 m radius (`railwayCourse`), the
sharpest the 60 cm lines took. One panel in six is worn, a sleeper gone and others knocked askew. Rakes of flat wagons
stand by each dump within 10 m of the line, loaded with shells, crates and sacks from the kit or standing empty, and the
line ends in a timber buffer stop. A wagon lies off the rails on its side partway along, its load spilled beside it,
kept off the roads. Where the line runs off the sector, its track runs on 300 m over the ground beyond (see
[the world beyond](#the-world-beyond)), its trees cleared, so it doesn't stop dead at the edge.

The track, wagons and stop are made in code, since no Synty pack has a railway; the loads are kit pieces under names of
their own, so they're drawn as far off as the wagons rather than dropped with the litter. Shellfire cuts the track and
throws the loads off; the wagons stand. The track casts no shadow worth its cost and stops drawing beyond 260 m, where
the mud of its bed carries the line. The simulation takes no notice of the railway: its bed is ordinary ground.

### Ground work

The ground is worked for what stands on it ([sculpt.ts](../packages/map/src/sculpt.ts), `GROUND_WORK`). It changes the
map's own heights and surfaces, so line of sight and movement see the ground the art stands on:

- **Levelled**: house plots and the landmark's yard to the middle of the ground under them (a median, so a shell hole on
  one side doesn't drag the level down), easing back into the slope; dumps, wrecks and garden walls likewise.
- **Dug in**: each field gun in a pit 0.7 m deep, its spoil thrown up into a berm round the front and sides and left
  open behind, where the crew came and went.
- **Banked**: earth heaped against the concrete posts to the loopholes.
- **Trodden**: churned earth round the ruins with limestone rubble in patches, and mud round the guns and dumps.
- **Graded**: the railway's bed levelled across, trodden to mud, and smoothed along its length to climb and fall no
  steeper than 1 in 25: midway between the highest such run under the ground and the lowest over it, so it's cut into
  rises and banked over dips no deeper than it must be (on the surveyed ground, up to about 1.2 m).

Nothing within about 1.7 m of a trench's centre line is touched, so every trench keeps its profile.

### Craters

Every shell hole has a ragged edge and lopsided spoil ([craters.ts](../packages/map/src/craters.ts)): its outline
wanders in and out of a circle in a few lobes, and a shell coming in at an angle throws most of its earth one way. Each
crater's shape is fixed by where it lands, so the simulation, which stamps craters too, always cuts the same one. After
the generator's bombardment, fresh walls and lips slump to about 45 degrees (`slumpGround`), so no wall stands vertical.

### Playing the ground

`node scripts/analyze-sector.mjs` marches sight lines over the height field from every defending machine gun and
concrete post, and reports how much of no man's land, and of each attack route, an attacker crosses under their eyes.
`--side french` analyses a German attack instead, and `--height 0.35` a man crawling. Standing, on the surveyed ground:

| Route                      | French attack | German attack |
| -------------------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| Up the Ravin de la Dame    | 6%            | 28%           |
| Along the sunken road      | 25%           | 20%           |
| Straight across the middle | 25%           | 71%           |
| Through Fleury             | 0%            | 3%            |

The real crater field is the biggest single source of cover: on generated ground the German guns see 78% of no man's
land, on the surveyed ground 57%, and 49% once the ruins, walls and trees block sight too (`--bare` leaves them out).
The ravine is the flanking route the sector is built around: deep enough, with steep enough banks, that only the post
sited at its head looks into it, so an attack up it has to deal with that post. A test keeps it that way. The ground
favours a French attack, which suits a player who fights French. Fleury is the other covered way across: its ruins hide
an attack up the main street almost entirely from both sides' guns, so it is fought for house by house, as it was
in 1916.

What blocks sight and walking is one table, `SCENERY_FOOTPRINTS` in [scenery.ts](../packages/map/src/scenery.ts): for
each kind of scenery, the ground it takes up, how high it stands, and what it costs to walk through. The simulation's
line of sight stands an upright cylinder of that height on each piece (a ruin 4.5 m, the landmark barn 7 m, a wall 1.3
m, a dead tree's trunk 8 m, an orchard's fruit tree 2.8 m; rubble 1.1 m hides only a man lying down), and its
pathfinding reads the same table.

The skirmish and the artillery find each side's front line as the fire trench facing the enemy that lies furthest
forward, so the layout keeps every front line spanning the map from west to east.

## Map format

The map stores what the simulation will need: heights, surfaces, trench polylines, and since format version 4,
**scenery** (ruins, trees, stumps, logs, guns, wrecks, bunkers, machine guns, knife rests, stores, bridges and pools,
each with a position, heading, variant and scale), **wire belts** (polylines) and **objectives** (named points with a
radius). Version 5 added the **weather region**, and version 6 the **railways** (polylines) and **fruit trees**. The
client picks the art; the map never names a Synty model. Pure decoration that follows from geometry, such as the trench
kit pieces, fire steps, duckboards and ladders, is derived by the client and isn't stored.

## Trenches and the trench kit

Synty's deep trench kit is modular: straight sections 3.22 m long with revetted walls 2.2 m apart, corners, T junctions,
end caps, fire steps. Two things make it fit:

- **Trench lines are traced for the kit** ([trench-trace.ts](../packages/map/src/trench-trace.ts)): straight runs and
  right-angle turns only, with at least 4.1 m of straight trench either side of every turn and junction for the kit's
  corner and junction pieces. Fire trenches use the crenellated square-wave trace of every Western Front trench map;
  communication trenches zigzag east and west as they run north.
- **The carved profile matches the kit** (`TRENCH_PROFILE`): walls at ±1.1 m, floor 1.9 m down, a fire step 0.85 m down
  from 0.35 m to 1.1 m in front of the centre line, and spoil lips peaking 0.4 m high 1.9 m out. Soldiers stand where
  the kit's floor and fire step are, and line of sight sees what the art shows.

The client lines every trench in [trench-dressing.ts](../apps/client/src/level/trench-dressing.ts): straight sections
scaled to fit each run, corners at turns, T junctions where a trench ends on another, end caps, fire steps along the
front wall of bays, duckboards, dugout entrances in the rear lines, ladders in the front line, a course of sandbags
along nearly every bay's parapet in the front line and most behind it (kept below the eye of a man on the fire step, so
the art matches line of sight), broken here and there where a shell blew a stretch in and left a heap of burst bags,
stores and tools against the walls, and telephone poles beside the communication trenches. Burst bags, planks, sheets of
iron, ammunition boxes, spare duckboards, spades and duds lie on the lips of spoil, behind a bay and either side of a
sap, so the kit's smooth lips don't read as pillows. The lines are lived in: rainwater stands on the floor under the
duckboards wherever the floor dips and in about a third of the rest, and the boards over it have settled into the mud,
each tipped its own way; every dugout's doorway has a gas curtain (a damp blanket, made in code since the kit has none);
braziers made from oil drums burn beside about half the dugouts with sparks rising from them; about three in five have a
stovepipe poking up through the earth over the room, wood smoke rising from it on the wind; washing hangs out to dry on
a line behind about one in three; and a signboard stands where each communication trench joins a fire trench. The
stovepipe and the washing are made in code too. A 0.5 m height grid can't follow vertical revetments, so once the kit is
placed the terrain is drawn slightly differently from the simulation's heights
([render-heights.ts](../apps/client/src/terrain/render-heights.ts)): pulled down under the kit's walls, floor and lips,
and darkened on the spoil so the seams between pieces read as shadow.

**Shellfire wrecks the props** ([battle-damage.ts](../apps/client/src/level/battle-damage.ts)). Every burst that makes a
crater reaches a little past the crater's bowl: duckboards, ladders, stores, poles and parapet sandbags within it are
destroyed, standing trees are shot down to stumps, and trench sections fill with blown-in earth and broken planks. The
ground itself is cratered by the simulation. The wire's art follows the simulation's wire
([wire-art.ts](../apps/client/src/level/wire-art.ts)): a fence piece is drawn while less than half the ground it covers
is cut through, a tangle while its own spot isn't, so the gaps an attack can go through show where the simulation has
them, and close again when wiring parties mend the wire by night. Debris is thrown down into spare slots its batches
keep, and batches switch to their far copies with a margin, so what's drawn changes rarely and a recorded frame
(snapshot rendering) replays for longer.

## Ground

The terrain is drawn from the simulation's height grid in 64 m chunks, one vertex a metre. The chunks around where the
camera looks, within about 90 m, are drawn at every 0.5 m sample instead, so the survey's full detail shows up close:
old shell holes, the lip of every crater, the ruts of the old streets. They are rebuilt a chunk a frame as the camera
pans, and a short skirt hangs below every chunk's edges to close the slivers where a detailed chunk meets a coarser one
and where the sector meets the backdrop. Each quad splits along the diagonal whose ends are nearer in height, which
follows ridges and crater walls instead of cutting across them in a staircase.

The ground's relief is baked into its shading as well as its shape: ground lying below the ground a metre or two round
it (crater bowls, the foot of a bank) sees less sky and darkens, crests and lips catch a little more light, and the
ground darkens at the foot of whatever stands on it (ruins, walls, trees, guns, wrecks).

The terrain is textured with four Synty ground layers (grass, mud, chalk, churned earth) blended per vertex by surface
([ground-splat-plugin.ts](../apps/client/src/terrain/ground-splat-plugin.ts), GLSL and WGSL). Layers blend by their
textures' brightness as well as their weights, so pebbles and clods poke through a neighbouring layer and edges come out
ragged; a second, rotated lookup breaks up the repeat. Each layer's normal map bends the light across the ground, and
muddy ground lying well below its surroundings is drawn darker and cooler, wet. The spoil beside trenches is darkened to
match the trench kit's earth. Steep ground (crater walls, the ravine's banks, the sunken road's sides) takes its colour
from a second lookup projected sideways, so textures don't smear down the slope. Grass tufts grow where the shelling
spared the grass, thickest where it's unbroken. Chalk is graded grey and kept at about half its texture's contrast: at
full contrast its white pebbles glared like snow.

### The world beyond

[backdrop.ts](../apps/client/src/terrain/backdrop.ts) draws the real ground round the sector out to the horizon: a 3 km
square of IGN's survey centred on the sector, every 5 m in
[fleury-surroundings.bin](../packages/map/data/fleury-surroundings.bin)
(`node scripts/fetch-elevation.mjs surroundings`), drawn every 10 m with the sector's own ground material. Within 60 m
of the sector it eases from the sector's edge heights into the survey, so the two meet without a step, and under the
sector it sinks out of sight. Shelled earth runs along the front's line east and west of the sector, and grass comes
back behind the lines, where woods of dead trees stand (thin instances of the kit's far tree models, a mesh for each
kind in each 750 m cell, so the camera draws only the cells it looks toward). Fog does the rest. The ravines that cut
the plateau east and west of Fleury, and Fort de Souville's outline to the south-east, are the survey's own.

All fourteen thousand trees had been three fifths of every frame's triangles, drawn whether the camera looked their way
or not, mostly as specks of a pixel or less. Now every tree stands within 500 m of the sector; beyond, the woods thin to
three in ten by 1.5 km, those left standing larger so the woods still cover the hills, and past 800 m only the plainer
tree models grow. Shadows reach none of the woods beyond 200 m, so those neither cast nor receive one. About 9,750 trees
are left, and the frame draws under a million of their triangles where it had drawn 3.8 million. Toward the horizon the
woods look the same.

## Shellfire

### The burst

Shells burst as the newsreels showed them ([shell-burst.ts](../apps/client/src/level/shell-burst.ts)): a white flash, a
fireball rolling up out of the ground, a column of earth thrown straight up and falling back (ten metres for a field
gun's shell, twenty-five for a heavy howitzer's), a ring of dust driven out along the ground, sparks, and black smoke
boiling up through the column and drifting off on the wind. Shrapnel bursts in the air as a white ball of smoke over a
patter of dust where the balls strike; gas shells burst with a dull pop and a greenish puff before the cloud takes over;
grenades make a small burst of their own. Each element is one pooled particle system for every burst of its size class,
emitting from a queue, so a barrage costs no new systems. The crater is the simulation's.

Babylon reads particle size gradients as the size itself, in metres, not as a factor of the system's size: smoke, dust
and the column set their sizes over life in metres and vary each particle by its scale.

### Its marks

What a shell leaves once the flash and the column of earth have gone
([shell-aftermath.ts](../apps/client/src/level/shell-aftermath.ts)):

- **Scorch**: the ground blackened in a faceted blot about the burst, a black core and a browner ring, with ragged
  splashes thrown out around it. Scorch is painted into a one-byte-per-metre texture over the whole map
  ([scorch-map.ts](../apps/client/src/level/scorch-map.ts)) that the ground material darkens by, so it survives the
  terrain being rebuilt under it. Rain washes it into the mud: heavy rain halves it every ten real minutes, about two
  hours of the game's day, drizzle more slowly and snow not at all.
- **Smoke** left hanging over shelled ground for half a minute, drifting with the simulation's wind.
- **Clods of earth** thrown out and falling back.
- **Fires**, now and then, burning for a minute or so with sparks rising: four times likelier where there's timber to
  catch (trees, ruins, stores, guns, wrecks).
- **A jolt of the camera** when a shell lands near what it's looking at, fading over most of a second.
- **Crows** on the wire in no man's land go up and circle when shells land near them, then settle on another stretch of
  wire ([trench-life.ts](../apps/client/src/level/trench-life.ts)).

## The miniature look

[post-process.ts](../apps/client/src/level/post-process.ts) runs the image through Babylon's default rendering pipeline:
4x multisampling, bloom on whatever burns brighter than lit chalk (flames, flashes, sparks), a little animated film
grain, and a depth of field that makes the battlefield read as a model on a table: sharp where the camera looks, soft
nearer and further off, which at the game's oblique angle is a tilt-shift band across the screen. No real lens blurs a
field 60 m away, so its focal length scales with the camera's distance, keeping the same shallow focus at every zoom.
The colour grade runs in the pipeline's image processing pass.

## Light and air

[atmosphere.ts](../apps/client/src/level/atmosphere.ts) sets a grey autumn afternoon:

- **Sun** about 30 degrees up in the west-south-west, warm, through thin overcast; a cool, dim sky. A low sun is what
  makes trench walls, craters and the shattered woods read from above.
- **Cascaded shadows**: two cascades out to 160 m, from the ground itself (craters, the ravine, trench walls) and from
  trees, ruins, guns, wrecks and soldiers. Small or flat props and the trench kit (already shadowed by the carved ground
  beneath it) don't cast. That keeps the cost at about 5 ms of CPU a frame at gameplay zoom. Once the camera is high
  enough that shadows would be sub-pixel, over 200 m up until it comes back below 180 m, the sun casts none and nothing
  receives any, with the shaders for that built while the battle loads. Engines without cascaded shadow maps go without.
  Babylon draws every caster into every cascade, so each cascade keeps only the casters in the sun's view of it
  (`castersInCascade` in [atmosphere.ts](../apps/client/src/level/atmosphere.ts)). The ground beyond the sector and its
  woods, three kilometres of them and seven tenths of every cascade's triangles, cast nothing, except the few hundred
  trees within 200 m of the sector, which the camera can look on from its edge. That took the shadow pass from about
  twelve million triangles a frame to under three at gameplay zoom, and the shadows' share of the frame from about 24 ms
  to 17 ms on an integrated GPU at 1080p, with the picture unchanged. The soldiers have no bounds to cull by, so the crowd
  measures how near the camera each of its layers' nearest man drawn comes, and a cascade ending nearer than that, by
  more than his shadow can fall toward the camera, leaves the layer out. In the play view the first cascade ends about
  20 m out and holds no ground, so no soldier is drawn into it. With the sun under 10 degrees they cast into both.
- **One colour grade**: desaturated, a touch of contrast, warm highlights, cool shadows, a light vignette.
- **Smoke** drifting east on the wind from about one ruin in four and every wreck, in Synty's faceted smoke sprite, and
  **mist** lying in the hollows. Smoke, dust and mist are drawn unlit, so they're lit by hand
  ([particle-light.ts](../apps/client/src/level/particle-light.ts)): each takes its midday colour times the fog's colour
  against midday's, so it darkens at night instead of glowing, and warms at sunset. Flames, flashes and sparks give off
  their own light and are left alone. Babylon leaves particles out of the scene's fog unless asked, so everything drawn
  over the scene (smoke, dust, earth, mist and flame) is set in it: left out, a burst's column of earth stood black
  against fog that hid the ground beneath it. Flashes and sparks add their light and stay out of the fog, shining
  through it as they would.
- **Soft edges** ([soft-particles.ts](../apps/client/src/level/soft-particles.ts)): a particle is a flat card facing the
  camera, so mist and smoke lying on the ground cut a hard line where their cards went into the earth. Each now fades
  out over the last eighth of its size before the ground behind it, read from the depth map the depth of field already
  draws. Babylon has no soft particles, so the fade is added to its own particle shaders behind a define, in GLSL and
  WGSL, and only the puffs that lie on the ground pay for it: not the earth, sparks and shrapnel flung through the air.
  A longer fade thins the mist, which lies close to the ground by design: a quarter of each puff cost up to a third of
  its brightness at the usual view, an eighth about a tenth.
- **In the low-lying fog** ([particle-fog.ts](../apps/client/src/level/particle-fog.ts)): where the scene has the
  weather's height fog ([height-fog.ts](../apps/client/src/environment/height-fog.ts), handed over with `useHeightFog`),
  every particle system set in fog takes it in place of Babylon's, which thickens with distance alone. They use the same
  function as the ground's shaders, so smoke fades into the haze as the ground under it does. Both additions to
  Babylon's particle shaders go through [particle-shaders.ts](../apps/client/src/level/particle-shaders.ts).

Screen-space ambient occlusion was tried in planning and left out: the shadows already take the frame budget it would
need, and the darkened spoil and wet hollows give much of the same grounding.

## Sound

Everything plays through one Web Audio graph, [mixer.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/mixer.ts). It has a battle bus, an
ambience bus, an "ears" bus for what is heard inside the head (ringing, breathing in a mask) and a music bus, which a
near miss never muffles. Audio starts on the player's first click or key press, as browsers require, and a sound that
won't decode is left out with a warning rather than stopping the game.

- **Distance and space.** Each battle sound is panned to its side of the screen, quieter further off (with separate near
  and far recordings), and delayed by the speed of sound, so a distant burst is seen before it is heard.
  - A low-pass takes the highs off with distance, as air does.
  - While the camera is close, sounds with the ground between (behind a rise, beyond the trench wall when the listener
    is down in the trench) lose more highs and some level, checked with the simulation's sight lines.
  - Down in a trench, in the controlled soldier's dugout or among Fleury's ruins, a reverb made in code colours what is
    heard: tight earthy walls, a small dead room, slapback off broken walls. It fades out as the camera climbs.
  - At most 48 battle sounds play at once. The least needed give way first: shouts, whistles and rounds cracking past
    over the fight close by, that over the far battle and footsteps; within each, the quietest.
- **Ambience** ([ambience.ts](../apps/client/src/level/ambience.ts)) crossfades four looping beds by where the camera
  looks: wind over no man's land with the guns rumbling in it, creaking shattered trees in the woods, crows over
  Fleury's ruins and birdsong behind the lines. A distant gun fires every few seconds from a random side. After dark the
  birdsong, crows and creaking trees fade.
- **The battle** ([battle-sound-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/battle-sound-model.ts)) turns each simulation step
  into sounds:
  - **Rifles** by side, the bolt worked after each shot nearby. The Mosin-Nagant 1891 stands in for the Lebel, and the
    M1917 Enfield and Springfield's Mauser-pattern actions for the Gewehr 98 (BOOM's World War I Firearms).
  - **Machine guns** round by round up close (Browning M1919, the Hotchkiss and MG 08's rate of fire), a recorded burst
    every 1.5 s from afar. Rounds from more than 40 m **crack** past the listener as they arrive, and zip by when they
    pass within 4 m. Where they land nearby is heard as the dirt or sparks fly (The Recordist's Bullets 3): a thud into
    earth, a thump into mud when the ground is sodden, a crump into snow, a splash in a shell hole's pool or a flooded
    trench, a crack off the ruins' stone, a ping off the wire, a thump into a parapet, and one in four a ricochet.
  - **Shells** by type, each with a near and a far recording. Field shells' crack and burst come together, as
    whizz-bangs gave no warning. Heavy 155 mm shells are ready to **rush in** for the last 2 s before landing within 150
    m, once a real recording of that rush is found (none is, so far). There are shrapnel airbursts, the quiet pop and
    hiss of gas shells, and grenades thrown and bursting. Earth **rains down** after a cratering burst within 35 m.
    **The batteries** fire 4 km behind each line, heard about 12 s late.
  - **Shell shock**: a burst within 15 m of the controlled soldier sets his ears ringing and muffles the battle and the
    ambience by up to 80%, coming back over 3 s.
  - **Men** cry out when hit and fall within reach. Squads roar as they go over the top, and the enemy's counter-attack
    is heard from its front line; men cheer a trench they've taken. These crowds and the cries of men hit are BOOM's
    Crowds – War & Battle, men only and wordless. The gas alarm (a struck shell case) sounds when men nearby start
    masking. The controlled soldier breathes through his mask, and short and hard while pinned down.
  - **Shouts**, in each man's own language, within 90 m: a squad's leader cries "En avant !" or "Sprung auf!" before the
    cheer, the nearest man masking cries the gas alarm, men near a shell landing shout to take cover (as a heavy shell
    is heard coming, just after a field shell), a thrower shouts his grenade, every second man hit calls out for a
    stretcher-bearer, and a man shouts as he is pinned down or cries out as his nerve breaks. A squad's leader shouts
    "follow me" or "hold" as he gives the order, men give themselves up with "Je me rends !" or "Kamerad!", and a man of
    the side taking an objective cheers it. One at a time, at most one every 1.5 s, and the same line at most once in 6
    s.
  - **Zero hour**: as squads are sent after a creeping barrage, up to three officers blow their whistles, and the cry to
    go and the cheer follow 1.2 s later (silent until a whistle is recorded). Raids go out without a sound.
  - **Footsteps** ([footstep-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/footstep-model.ts)): with the camera low, men standing
    within 18 m step once a stride in boots, walking or running (Pro Sound Effects' Game Audio Collection: Footsteps):
    on the duckboards along a trench floor, through shallow water in a shell hole's pool or a trench flooded after rain,
    on snow, the ruins' rubble, mud where the map has mud or the ground is sodden, grass, and the crunch of the
    shell-torn ground and chalk. A running man's kit rattles.
- **Weather and the front** ([atmosphere-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/atmosphere-model.ts)):
  - Rain falls as hard as the weather says (snow is silent), dripping on corrugated iron down in a trench, and the wind
    gusts once it blows harder than a breeze, the wire's belts singing in it close by.
  - At night, crickets and owls when it's warm and dry, the wind through dark woods when it's cold.
  - Thunder follows each lightning flash at about 3 s a kilometre.
  - As the camera climbs, the detail gives way to the whole front's guns from far off, louder the heavier the recent
    fighting.
- **The level's own sounds** ([level-sound-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/level-sound-model.ts)): braziers and
  burning wrecks crackle as the camera nears them, and crows caw as a flock lifts off the wire.
- **Music** ([music-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/music-model.ts)): the soundtrack is our own, composed for the
  game. It opens on the theme, _La Verdun_. After that, when a track ends and 25–70 s of silence have let the front be
  heard, the next is chosen for the player's squad's mood: _waiting_ in a quiet trench by day, _dread_ under heavy
  fighting or after dark, _attack_ once the squad is over the top (behind the barrage, on a raid, or sent out of the
  trench). Zero hour is the one thing that cuts a track short: the whistle fades in an attack track over 2.5 s, and
  attack tracks follow one another without a pause until the squad is back in a trench. Each mood plays all its tracks
  before repeating one. The tracks stream rather than decode up front.

The sounds come from Pole Position's Warfare Library, BOOM's World War I Firearms and Crowds – War & Battle, The
Recordist's Bullets 3, Super Thump's Weapons of World War II and the Sonniss GDC bundles, recorded by professionals:
designed stand-ins and weak sampler sounds were taken out, and sounds with no good recording stay silent
([sound](sound.md) lists them). The ringing and the reverbs are generated. The shouts were generated with ElevenLabs
(`scripts/generate-voices.mjs`, lines in `scripts/voice-lines.json`), and each take was chosen by ear: the build uses
only those listed under `keep`. `scripts/build-sound-effects.py` cuts them:

- It finds each take in the long source recordings and cuts it until it dies away.
- It loops beds with a crossfade, and cuts the loudest stretches of recordings that never fall quiet.
- It matches each sound's variations in loudness, with a limiter catching the peaks.

```
pnpm sound:fetch
blender -b --factory-startup -P scripts/build-sound-effects.py -- vendor/audio .asset-cache/level
```

The libraries live in the asset library on E: (`docs/assets/backup.md`), not the checkout. `pnpm sound:fetch` asks the
build which recordings it reads (`-- --sources`, 282 files, 2.6 GB of the libraries' 100 GB) and copies just those into
`vendor/audio`. Each Ogg gets a serial number from its name, so a sound rebuilt the same is byte for byte the same, and
git sees only the sounds that changed.

It writes mono Opus files and a manifest to `.asset-cache/level/sfx/`, about 9 MB (long loops at a lower bitrate),
swapping them in file by file. The asset pack encrypts them with the rest of the kit. The ambience beds come from the
kit build's `audio` section.

The music's WAV masters live in `vendor/audio/music/` (named for their titles: the model knows each by its slug, such as
`trench-dread`). `pnpm sound:music` levels each to -20 LUFS with ffmpeg's two-pass loudnorm and encodes it to stereo
Opus at 112 kbps in `.asset-cache/music/`, about 31 MB for 37 minutes. Being ours, it ships raw beside the bundle under
`music/` rather than in the encrypted pack; without it the game plays no music. What's still missing (recordings, game
hooks and sound design) is listed in [sound](sound.md).

## Level kit

`scripts/build-level-kit.py` builds the kit from the converted Synty packs (see the
[asset pipeline](assets/pipeline.md)):

```
blender -b --factory-startup -P scripts/build-level-kit.py -- .asset-cache/unity vendor/unity .asset-cache/level
```

It writes `.asset-cache/level/kit.glb`: about 100 pieces from War Map WWI, War Pack, Nature and the generic props,
joined one mesh per piece, sharing 1K palettes, with a decimated far-distance copy of every piece over 700 triangles. It
also writes four ground textures from the Nature pack with their normal maps, graded so their mean colours match the
terrain's flat palette colours; effect sprites from the Particle FX pack (smoke, mist, a flipbook of flames, a spark,
and a faceted dark blot for scorch and flying earth); and the ambience, cut and encoded with Blender's audio module.

The dev server serves the raw kit at `/level-kit/`. **Production builds never ship it raw**: paid assets ship only
inside the encrypted asset pack below ([licensing](assets/licensing.md)). Without the kit the game falls back to the
greybox: flat-coloured terrain with plain carved trenches.

In the game, placements are drawn in batches ([scenery-batches.ts](../apps/client/src/level/scenery-batches.ts)): every
placement of a kit piece within a 128 m cell of the map is a thin instance of one mesh, or within the whole map for a
piece placed fewer than about fifty times. Each batch has a Geometry of its own over the piece's vertex and index
buffers, which aren't copied: Babylon keeps thin instances in the Geometry, so batches sharing one would draw each
other's. The sector's 15,500 props make about 700 batches, so Babylon, which tests every mesh against the view every
frame (again for each shadow cascade and for the depth of field's depth pass), tests a few hundred meshes instead of
15,500. Heavy pieces switch to their far copy beyond 110 m and small clutter such as duckboards and splinters stops
drawing beyond 50–220 m, both measured to the nearest point of the batch, so pieces near the camera always draw. Grass,
litter, planks and the like cast no shadows. The depth of field's depth map is drawn at half the screen's size and holds
only the ground: looking down on the battlefield, what stands on it is near enough the ground's depth, and every prop
drawn again would cost a draw call. Batches of grass and trees carry `metadata.sway`, how freely they bend in the wind
(1 for grass, 0.25 for a crowned tree, 0.1 for a pole), for the wind's sway on the kit's materials. Zoomed fully out the
sector draws about 1.6 M triangles.

### Asset pack

`pnpm assets:pack` (`scripts/build-asset-pack.mjs`) packs every file under `.asset-cache/level/`, keyed by its path
relative to it (`kit.glb`, `ground/mud.png`, `fx/smoke.png`), into `.asset-cache/pack/level.pack`, and writes the key to
`.asset-cache/pack/level.key.json`. Rerun it after every kit build. Both files are git-ignored; never commit them.

The pack is a custom container: a 32-byte header (magic `8E "VDNPK" 0D 0A`, format version, index length and IV), an
encrypted index of `{ path, offset, length, iv, encoding }` entries, then the files. Everything after the header is
AES-256-GCM under a key drawn fresh on every run. Each file has its own IV and is bound to its path; the index is bound
to the header. Tampering with any byte makes decryption fail, and nothing in the pack is readable without the key.

Encrypted bytes don't compress, so the web server's own compression can't shrink the pack. Instead each file is gzipped
before it's encrypted, when that saves a tenth or more, and its entry says `encoding: "gzip"`. The two meshes shrink to
about a third (`kit.glb` 13.1 MiB to 5.1 MiB, `soldier.glb` 4.3 MiB to 1.5 MiB). The sounds and images are compressed
already and go in as they are. The pack came to 20.6 MiB from 31.4 MiB of files. Format version 2 added the encoding;
the reader still opens version 1 packs.

`pnpm -F @verdun/client build` picks the pack up if it exists: it emits it as `assets/<content hash>.bin` and bakes its
URL and the base64 key into the bundle as `import.meta.env.VERDUN_LEVEL_PACK_URL` and `_KEY` (the `assetPack` plugin in
`apps/client/vite.config.ts`). In the browser, `src/level/kit-files.ts` starts a module worker
(`src/assets/pack-worker.ts`) that fetches the pack once, decrypts the index with WebCrypto, and decrypts (and inflates)
each file on request, handing it to the page without a copy. It reads the pack as it downloads, answering each request
as soon as that file's bytes are in. The pack script puts the sounds last, so the level is built from the meshes and
images while the sounds are still coming, and the battle waits for them only at its last loading step. The menus open
the kit's files as the page loads (`src/main.ts`), so the pack, most of what the battle downloads, comes down beside the
battle's own code rather than after the renderer, the ground and the simulation have started. The console logs
`Level kit: opened the asset pack` when it opens. With no pack the build still succeeds, as in CI, and the game stays
greybox.

The key ships in the bundle, so the pack stops casual extraction, not a determined one. Whether that counts as
"reasonable measures" is on the licensing doc's legal-review list; signed, short-lived pack URLs come with a server.

In dev, `?pack` reads the pack through the worker instead of the raw `/level-kit/` route: the dev server serves it under
`/level-pack/`. To try a production build, run `pnpm assets:pack`, then `pnpm -F @verdun/client build` and
`pnpm -F @verdun/client preview` (the `client-preview` launch configuration).

The level kit is committed, through Git LFS (`.gitattributes`), so CI builds the real game: it fetches the kit, runs
`pnpm assets:pack` and builds with the pack under a fresh key, as a deploy would. Commit the kit's files with the change
that rebuilds them.

## Sector files

Each sector's features live in a Tiled map: [packages/map/sectors/fleury.json](../packages/map/sectors/fleury.json).
Tiled opens JSON maps as they are, and the game, the simulation's worker and the server import the same file. The
designer lays out the lie of the ground and what stands on it; the generator fills in the rest (trench bays, shell
holes, scenery, surfaces) round them. [sector-file.ts](../packages/map/src/sector-file.ts) reads and writes the format.

It's in the frame described under Editing in Tiled below: one-metre tiles at 10 px a metre, y flipped so north is up.
The map's size is the sector's, and its properties hold its `name`, `weatherRegion` and `rise`, the generated ground's
height south to north as `z:height` pairs. One object layer for each kind of feature, each object's class saying what it
is:

| Layer       | Objects                                                                                                                                                                             |
| ----------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `survey`    | an image layer, locked: the surveyed ground as shaded relief to lay out on, drawn by `pnpm map:backdrop`                                                                            |
| `ground`    | `ravine` and `sunken-road` polylines (`halfWidth`, `depth`), `village` circles (`height`), `crater` circles (`depth`)                                                               |
| `woods`     | `wood` ellipses, named, with a `density` from 0 to 1                                                                                                                                |
| `roads`     | `road` and `street` polylines (the street named `main street` first), a `crossroads` point; the layer's `frenchRear` and `germanRear` properties are where roads keep their surface |
| `railways`  | `railway` polylines along the track, `dump` points                                                                                                                                  |
| `landmarks` | `church` points (`heading` in degrees, `variant`), `orchard` points at the south-west tree (`rows`, `trees`, `spacing`, `jitter`)                                                   |

New sectors are 1 km wide along the front and 800 m deep; Fleury, the first, stays 600 m by 400 m until it's remade.
Their trench lines are traced from the trench maps of 1916: the sources and how to trace from them are in
[historical maps](historical-maps.md).

Fleury's file holds what fleury.ts used to: the map it generates is the same to the byte. Its trench lines, batteries
and objectives are still worked out in code; drawing the trench lines in the file comes next, and with it sectors traced
from the trench maps of 1916.

## Editing in Tiled

The layout (trench lines, wire belts, railways and scenery) can be edited in [Tiled](https://www.mapeditor.org), as the
[editor plan](tooling.md#editor-plan) proposes. [tiled.ts](../packages/map/src/tiled.ts) converts between our map format
and a Tiled JSON map (`.tmj`). Heights, surfaces and craters stay generated.

```
pnpm map:export-tiled                                 # writes maps/fleury.tmj from generateFleurySector()
node scripts/export-tiled.mjs --check maps/fleury.tmj # reads an edited map and lists kit warnings
```

**Conventions.** An orthogonal map with no tilesets and one-metre tiles at 10 px per metre, so the grid and snapping
step is 1 m and Tiled's tile coordinates read as metres. Tiled's x is our x × 10. Tiled's y grows downward and our z
grows north, so y is flipped about the bottom edge: y = (depth − z) × 10, with depth the map's height in metres. North
is up on screen. The exporter rounds positions to the millimetre, headings to 0.01° and scales to 0.0001, and writes
keys in Tiled's alphabetical order with one object per line, so a moved tree is a one-line diff. Once Tiled re-saves the
file it uses its own layout.

| Layer        | Objects   | Data                                                                                         |
| ------------ | --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `trenches`   | Polylines | String properties `kind` (`fire` or `communication`) and `facing` (`north`, `south`, `none`) |
| `wire`       | Polylines | One belt each                                                                                |
| `scenery`    | Points    | Class (Tiled's object type) is the kind; float `heading`, float `scale`, int `variant`       |
| `railways`   | Polylines | One line each; its corners are eased into curves                                             |
| `objectives` | Points    | The object's name is the objective's; float `radius` in metres, the ground held round it     |

Headings are in **degrees** clockwise from north, which is clockwise on screen (the map format stores radians). A
scenery point without properties gets heading 0, scale 1 and variant 0. Communication trenches may leave `facing` out.
Copying an existing object is the quickest way to get its properties. A rotated polyline is read with its rotation
applied. Other layers, such as a reference image a designer adds, are ignored.

**Validation.** `fromTiledMap` throws a `TypeError` naming the Tiled object and layer when something can't be read: a
missing layer, a rectangle where a polyline belongs, an unknown kind or class, a fire trench without a facing, a
negative scale. Places the trench kit can't line are warnings instead, listed by object:

- turns that aren't right angles;
- less than `TRENCH_GRID.clearance` (4.1 m) of straight trench between a free end and a turn, or twice that between two
  turns or junctions;
- a trench ending on another at an angle (a junction must be square: a T, or a corner where it meets another's end).

A trench whose end lies within 5 cm of another trench's centre line joins it there. The generated Fleury sector has no
warnings, and a test keeps it that way.

**Using an edited layout.** `generateFleurySector({ layout: fromTiledMap(json) })` builds the sector around a Tiled
layout: its trenches, wire, scenery, objectives and railways replace the generated ones, while heights, surfaces, roads
and craters stay seeded. A map saved before there was an `objectives` or `railways` layer keeps the generated objectives
or railway. Pools and bridges follow the craters and roads, so they are always regenerated. Each side's front line must
still span the map from west to east. Client and simulation must load the same layout.

## Dev tools

- `?webgl` forces WebGL2 in dev builds, to test the GLSL shaders. It also helps automated checks when the browser pane
  is hidden: a hidden page gets no animation frames, but under WebGL `view.scene.render()` followed by the canvas's
  `toDataURL()` still captures a frame.
- `window.verdun.level` holds the batched scenery (`scenery.batches`, `scenery.near(x, z, radius)`), the placements, any
  pieces the kit was missing, the atmosphere, the shell damage and the ambience.
- **Death map**: J toggles a faint red disc wherever a soldier has fallen. Discs pile into solid red on the killing
  grounds. The record survives reloads, so it builds up over a playtest; `window.verdun.deaths.clear()` starts afresh
  and `points()` exports it.
- `node scripts/analyze-sector.mjs`: dead ground and exposure, as above.
- `node scripts/fetch-elevation.mjs`: downloads the surveyed ground again.
