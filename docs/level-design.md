# Level design

The prototype battlefield is the **Fleury sector**, generated by `generateFleurySector` in
[packages/map/src/fleury.ts](../packages/map/src/fleury.ts) and dressed with Synty art from the level kit. This doc
covers how the level is laid out, how the trench kit fits the simulation's terrain, and how the art reaches the game.

## The Fleury sector

600 m × 400 m of the real ground at Fleury-devant-Douaumont, north-east of Verdun, fought over through the summer
of 1916. The terrain is surveyed: IGN's LiDAR HD bare-earth model, which a century on still shows the shell holes of the
battle. The village sits on its real site, on the high ground in the east, with the ground falling some 48 m to the
west. The trench lines, the ravine and the sunken road are laid out for play on top of it.

Beyond the sector's edges the real ground goes on to the horizon (see [the world beyond](#the-world-beyond)), so the
battlefield sits in a landscape rather than on a table. [The level audit](level-audit.md) records how the sector
measured against a AAA bar and the overhaul that followed.

### Real ground

`node scripts/fetch-elevation.mjs` downloads the sector's window of IGN's LiDAR HD terrain model (MNT, 0.5 m, the
simulation's own resolution) from the Géoplateforme and writes
[packages/map/data/fleury-elevation.bin](../packages/map/data/fleury-elevation.bin): 1201 × 801 samples in centimetres
above a base height, about 1.9 MB (format in [elevation.ts](../packages/map/src/elevation.ts)). The window's south-west
corner is Lambert-93 876773 E, 6902290 N. The data is © IGN under Licence Ouverte 2.0: it ships raw, with the credit
"IGN – LiDAR HD" (see the [register](assets/register.md)).

`generateFleurySector({ elevation })` builds the sector on it: surveyed heights replace the generated rises and plateau,
the ravine and sunken road are cut into them, and about 1,500 fresh shell holes go on top of the old crater field
instead of 2,600. Mud, chalk and water follow the survey's own spread of heights. Without `elevation` the generator
still makes its synthetic ground, which the tests use.

| Feature                   | Where                         | Why it's there                                                                   |
| ------------------------- | ----------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| French lines              | South, front line at z ≈ 126  | The player's side, on the lower ground                                           |
| German lines              | North, front line at z ≈ 270  | Holding the rise toward the ridge                                                |
| Bois de la Caillette      | West, French side             | Shattered wood the French front runs through: cover, broken sightlines           |
| Ravin de la Dame          | North-west into no man's land | Dead ground for a flanking attack; only a concrete post at its head sees into it |
| Fleury                    | East, on a plateau            | Ruins where the lines close to 80 m; a French salient pushes into it             |
| Sunken road, mine craters | Middle of no man's land       | Cover for an attack across the widest stretch (about 140 m)                      |
| Chalk roads               | Rear areas and through Fleury | The supply roads, bridged where they cross trenches                              |
| Batteries                 | Behind both reserve lines     | Field guns in sandbagged pits                                                    |

Each side has three crenellated fire trenches (front, support, reserve), zigzag communication trenches between them,
saps pushed out to listening posts with machine guns and knife rests at their mouths, more machine guns on the
front-line parapet, supply dumps, and belts of wire: two French belts at about 22 m and 33 m in front of the line, three
German belts at 18, 30 and 42 m. Belts have staggered patrol gaps, each with a knife rest ready to close it, and gaps at
the sap heads. No man's land is shelled into churned ground with some 2,600 craters, the deeper ones holding water;
craters on the ridge throw up rims of chalk, and shelling tapers off behind the lines.

**Objectives**, the ground worth fighting for: Fleury, the Fleury crossroads, the mine craters, the Ravin de la Dame and
the ravine head. They're data for the capture rules; holding them is up to the simulation.

### The village

Fleury stands where the survey still shows it. A century of forest hasn't hidden its streets: the main street is a
smooth band running down the plateau from the German line in the north-west to the French line in the south-east, met by
a cross street near the French wire, with a lane out to the sunken road. The village is laid out on them
([fleury.ts](../packages/map/src/fleury.ts), `placeVillage`): a house on each 11 m plot either side, gable to the
street, with garden walls between the plots; a plot a big shell hole has taken holds only a heap of rubble, and a few
were yards to begin with. Rubble lies heaped against the ruins' walls. The landmark, seen from anywhere in the sector,
is the great barn of the farm on the main street with its chimney stack still standing, on the plot the shells reached
least. Fleury had a church, but the kit has none, and a barn and chimney read better from above than a church improvised
from house ruins. The village is all in no man's land, as it was in July 1916; the crossroads is an objective. No house
stands within 14 m of the map's edge.

### Ground work

The ground is worked for what stands on it ([sculpt.ts](../packages/map/src/sculpt.ts), `GROUND_WORK`). It changes the
map's own heights and surfaces, so line of sight and movement see the ground the art stands on:

- **Levelled**: house plots and the landmark's yard to the middle of the ground under them (a median, so a shell hole on
  one side doesn't drag the level down), easing back into the slope; dumps, wrecks and garden walls likewise.
- **Dug in**: each field gun in a pit 0.7 m deep, its spoil thrown up into a berm round the front and sides and left
  open behind, where the crew came and went.
- **Banked**: earth heaped against the concrete posts to the loopholes.
- **Trodden**: churned earth round the ruins with limestone rubble in patches, and mud round the guns and dumps.

Nothing within about 1.7 m of a trench's centre line is touched, so every trench keeps its profile.

### Craters

Every shell hole has a ragged edge and lopsided spoil ([craters.ts](../packages/map/src/craters.ts)): its outline
wanders in and out of a circle in a few lobes, and a shell coming in at an angle throws most of its earth one way. Each
crater's shape is fixed by where it lands, so the simulation, which stamps craters too, always cuts the same one. After
the generator's bombardment, fresh walls and lips slump to about 45 degrees (`slumpGround`), so no wall stands vertical.

### Playing the ground

`node scripts/analyze-sector.mjs` marches sight lines over the height field from every defending machine gun and
concrete post, and reports how much of no man's land, and of each attack route, an attacker crosses under their eyes.
`--side french` analyses a German attack instead, and `--height 0.35` a man crawling. Standing, on the surveyed ground:

| Route                      | French attack | German attack |
| -------------------------- | ------------- | ------------- |
| Up the Ravin de la Dame    | 6%            | 28%           |
| Along the sunken road      | 25%           | 20%           |
| Straight across the middle | 25%           | 71%           |
| Through Fleury             | 0%            | 3%            |

The real crater field is the biggest single source of cover: on generated ground the German guns see 78% of no man's
land, on the surveyed ground 57%, and 49% once the ruins, walls and trees block sight too (`--bare` leaves them out).
The ravine is the flanking route the sector is built around: deep enough, with steep enough banks, that only the post
sited at its head looks into it, so an attack up it has to deal with that post. A test keeps it that way. The ground
favours a French attack, which suits a player who fights French. Fleury is the other covered way across: its ruins hide
an attack up the main street almost entirely from both sides' guns, so it is fought for house by house, as it was
in 1916.

What blocks sight and walking is one table, `SCENERY_FOOTPRINTS` in [scenery.ts](../packages/map/src/scenery.ts): for
each kind of scenery, the ground it takes up, how high it stands, and what it costs to walk through. The simulation's
line of sight stands an upright cylinder of that height on each piece (a ruin 4.5 m, the landmark barn 7 m, a wall 1.3
m, a dead tree's trunk 8 m; rubble 1.1 m hides only a man lying down), and its pathfinding reads the same table.

The skirmish and the artillery find each side's front line as the fire trench facing the enemy that lies furthest
forward, so the layout keeps every front line spanning the map from west to east.

## Map format

The map stores what the simulation will need: heights, surfaces, trench polylines, and since format version 4,
**scenery** (ruins, trees, stumps, logs, guns, wrecks, bunkers, machine guns, knife rests, stores, bridges and pools,
each with a position, heading, variant and scale), **wire belts** (polylines) and **objectives** (named points with a
radius). The client picks the art; the map never names a Synty model. Pure decoration that follows from geometry, such
as the trench kit pieces, fire steps, duckboards and ladders, is derived by the client and isn't stored.

## Trenches and the trench kit

Synty's deep trench kit is modular: straight sections 3.22 m long with revetted walls 2.2 m apart, corners, T junctions,
end caps, fire steps. Two things make it fit:

- **Trench lines are traced for the kit** ([trench-trace.ts](../packages/map/src/trench-trace.ts)): straight runs and
  right-angle turns only, with at least 4.1 m of straight trench either side of every turn and junction for the kit's
  corner and junction pieces. Fire trenches use the crenellated square-wave trace of every Western Front trench map;
  communication trenches zigzag east and west as they run north.
- **The carved profile matches the kit** (`TRENCH_PROFILE`): walls at ±1.1 m, floor 1.9 m down, a fire step 0.85 m down
  from 0.35 m to 1.1 m in front of the centre line, and spoil lips peaking 0.4 m high 1.9 m out. Soldiers stand where
  the kit's floor and fire step are, and line of sight sees what the art shows.

The client lines every trench in [trench-dressing.ts](../apps/client/src/level/trench-dressing.ts): straight sections
scaled to fit each run, corners at turns, T junctions where a trench ends on another, end caps, fire steps along the
front wall of bays, duckboards, dugout entrances in the rear lines, ladders in the front line, a course of sandbags
along about half the bays' parapets (kept below the eye line of a man on the fire step, so the art matches line of
sight), stores and tools against the walls, and telephone poles beside the communication trenches. The lines are lived
in: rainwater stands on the floor under the duckboards wherever the floor dips and in about a third of the rest, every
dugout's doorway has a gas curtain (a damp blanket, made in code since the kit has none), braziers made from oil drums
burn beside about half the dugouts with sparks rising from them, and a signboard stands where each communication trench
joins a fire trench. A 0.5 m height grid can't follow vertical revetments, so once the kit is placed the terrain is
drawn slightly differently from the simulation's heights
([render-heights.ts](../apps/client/src/terrain/render-heights.ts)): pulled down under the kit's walls, floor and lips,
and darkened on the spoil so the seams between pieces read as shadow.

**Shellfire wrecks the props** ([battle-damage.ts](../apps/client/src/level/battle-damage.ts)). Every burst that makes a
crater reaches a little past the crater's bowl: wire, duckboards, ladders, stores, poles and parapet sandbags within it
are destroyed, standing trees are shot down to stumps, and trench sections fill with blown-in earth and broken planks.
The ground itself is cratered by the simulation. Wire is visual only so far; when the simulation tracks cut wire, the
art will follow it.

## Ground

The terrain is drawn from the simulation's height grid in 64 m chunks, one vertex a metre. The chunks around where the
camera looks, within about 90 m, are drawn at every 0.5 m sample instead, so the survey's full detail shows up close:
old shell holes, the lip of every crater, the ruts of the old streets. They are rebuilt a chunk a frame as the camera
pans, and a short skirt hangs below every chunk's edges to close the slivers where a detailed chunk meets a coarser one
and where the sector meets the backdrop. Each quad splits along the diagonal whose ends are nearer in height, which
follows ridges and crater walls instead of cutting across them in a staircase.

The ground's relief is baked into its shading as well as its shape: ground lying below the ground a metre or two round
it (crater bowls, the foot of a bank) sees less sky and darkens, crests and lips catch a little more light, and the
ground darkens at the foot of whatever stands on it (ruins, walls, trees, guns, wrecks).

The terrain is textured with four Synty ground layers (grass, mud, chalk, churned earth) blended per vertex by surface
([ground-splat-plugin.ts](../apps/client/src/terrain/ground-splat-plugin.ts), GLSL and WGSL). Layers blend by their
textures' brightness as well as their weights, so pebbles and clods poke through a neighbouring layer and edges come out
ragged; a second, rotated lookup breaks up the repeat. Each layer's normal map bends the light across the ground, and
muddy ground lying well below its surroundings is drawn darker and cooler, wet. The spoil beside trenches is darkened to
match the trench kit's earth. Steep ground (crater walls, the ravine's banks, the sunken road's sides) takes its colour
from a second lookup projected sideways, so textures don't smear down the slope. Grass tufts grow where the shelling
spared the grass, thickest where it's unbroken. Chalk is graded grey and kept at about half its texture's contrast: at
full contrast its white pebbles glared like snow.

### The world beyond

[backdrop.ts](../apps/client/src/terrain/backdrop.ts) draws the real ground round the sector out to the horizon: a 3 km
square of IGN's survey centred on the sector, every 5 m in
[fleury-surroundings.bin](../packages/map/data/fleury-surroundings.bin)
(`node scripts/fetch-elevation.mjs surroundings`), drawn every 10 m with the sector's own ground material. Within 60 m
of the sector it eases from the sector's edge heights into the survey, so the two meet without a step, and under the
sector it sinks out of sight. Shelled earth runs along the front's line east and west of the sector, and grass comes
back behind the lines, where woods of dead trees stand (thin instances of the kit's far tree models: one draw per kind
of tree, none culled one by one). Fog does the rest. The ravines that cut the plateau east and west of Fleury, and Fort
de Souville's outline to the south-east, are the survey's own.

## Shellfire

### The burst

Shells burst as the newsreels showed them ([shell-burst.ts](../apps/client/src/level/shell-burst.ts)): a white flash, a
fireball rolling up out of the ground, a column of earth thrown straight up and falling back (ten metres for a field
gun's shell, twenty-five for a heavy howitzer's), a ring of dust driven out along the ground, sparks, and black smoke
boiling up through the column and drifting off on the wind. Shrapnel bursts in the air as a white ball of smoke over a
patter of dust where the balls strike; gas shells burst with a dull pop and a greenish puff before the cloud takes over;
grenades make a small burst of their own. Each element is one pooled particle system for every burst of its size class,
emitting from a queue, so a barrage costs no new systems. The crater is the simulation's.

Babylon reads particle size gradients as the size itself, in metres, not as a factor of the system's size: smoke, dust
and the column set their sizes over life in metres and vary each particle by its scale.

### Its marks

What a shell leaves once the flash and the column of earth have gone
([shell-aftermath.ts](../apps/client/src/level/shell-aftermath.ts)):

- **Scorch**: the ground blackened in a faceted blot about the burst, a black core and a browner ring, with ragged
  splashes thrown out around it. Scorch is painted into a one-byte-per-metre texture over the whole map
  ([scorch-map.ts](../apps/client/src/level/scorch-map.ts)) that the ground material darkens by, so it survives the
  terrain being rebuilt under it.
- **Smoke** left hanging over shelled ground for half a minute, drifting with the simulation's wind.
- **Clods of earth** thrown out and falling back.
- **Fires**, now and then, burning for a minute or so with sparks rising: four times likelier where there's timber to
  catch (trees, ruins, stores, guns, wrecks).
- **A jolt of the camera** when a shell lands near what it's looking at, fading over most of a second.
- **Crows** on the wire in no man's land go up and circle when shells land near them, then settle on another stretch of
  wire ([trench-life.ts](../apps/client/src/level/trench-life.ts)).

## The miniature look

[post-process.ts](../apps/client/src/level/post-process.ts) runs the image through Babylon's default rendering pipeline:
4x multisampling, bloom on whatever burns brighter than lit chalk (flames, flashes, sparks), a little animated film
grain, and a depth of field that makes the battlefield read as a model on a table: sharp where the camera looks, soft
nearer and further off, which at the game's oblique angle is a tilt-shift band across the screen. No real lens blurs a
field 60 m away, so its focal length scales with the camera's distance, keeping the same shallow focus at every zoom.
The colour grade runs in the pipeline's image processing pass.

## Light and air

[atmosphere.ts](../apps/client/src/level/atmosphere.ts) sets a grey autumn afternoon:

- **Sun** about 30 degrees up in the west-south-west, warm, through thin overcast; a cool, dim sky. A low sun is what
  makes trench walls, craters and the shattered woods read from above.
- **Cascaded shadows**: two cascades out to 160 m, from the ground itself (craters, the ravine, trench walls) and from
  trees, ruins, guns, wrecks and soldiers. Small or flat props and the trench kit (already shadowed by the carved ground
  beneath it) don't cast, and nothing does once the camera is high enough that shadows would be sub-pixel. That keeps
  the cost at about 5 ms of CPU a frame at gameplay zoom. Engines without cascaded shadow maps go without.
- **One colour grade**: desaturated, a touch of contrast, warm highlights, cool shadows, a light vignette.
- **Smoke** drifting east on the wind from about one ruin in four and every wreck, in Synty's faceted smoke sprite, and
  **mist** lying in the hollows.

Screen-space ambient occlusion was tried in planning and left out: the shadows already take the frame budget it would
need, and the darkened spoil and wet hollows give much of the same grounding.

## Sound

Everything plays through one Web Audio graph, [mixer.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/mixer.ts). It has a battle bus, an
ambience bus and an "ears" bus for what is heard inside the head (ringing, breathing in a mask). Audio starts on the
player's first click or key press, as browsers require, and a sound that won't decode is left out with a warning rather
than stopping the game.

- **Distance and space.** Each battle sound is panned to its side of the screen, quieter further off (with separate near
  and far recordings), and delayed by the speed of sound, so a distant burst is seen before it is heard.
  - A low-pass takes the highs off with distance, as air does.
  - While the camera is close, sounds with the ground between (behind a rise, beyond the trench wall when the listener
    is down in the trench) lose more highs and some level, checked with the simulation's sight lines.
  - Down in a trench, in the controlled soldier's dugout or among Fleury's ruins, a reverb made in code colours what is
    heard: tight earthy walls, a small dead room, slapback off broken walls. It fades out as the camera climbs.
  - At most 48 battle sounds play at once; the quietest gives way.
- **Ambience** ([ambience.ts](../apps/client/src/level/ambience.ts)) crossfades four looping beds by where the camera
  looks: wind over no man's land with the guns rumbling in it, creaking shattered trees in the woods, crows over
  Fleury's ruins and birdsong behind the lines. A distant gun fires every few seconds from a random side.
- **The battle** ([battle-sound-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/battle-sound-model.ts)) turns each simulation step
  into sounds:
  - **Rifles** by side, the bolt worked after each shot nearby. The Mosin-Nagant 1891 stands in for the Lebel, and the
    M1917 Enfield and Springfield's Mauser-pattern actions for the Gewehr 98 (BOOM's World War I Firearms).
  - **Machine guns** round by round up close (Browning M1919, the Hotchkiss and MG 08's rate of fire), a recorded burst
    every 1.5 s from afar. Rounds from more than 40 m **crack** past the listener and thud into the ground nearby.
  - **Shells** by type, each with a near and a far recording. Field shells' crack and burst come together, as
    whizz-bangs gave no warning. Heavy 155 mm shells **rush in** for the last 2 s before landing within 150 m. There are
    shrapnel airbursts, the quiet pop and hiss of gas shells, and grenades thrown and bursting. Earth **rains down**
    after a cratering burst within 35 m. **The batteries** fire 4 km behind each line, heard about 12 s late.
  - **Shell shock**: a burst within 15 m of the controlled soldier sets his ears ringing and muffles the battle and the
    ambience by up to 80%, coming back over 3 s.
  - **Men** cry out when hit and fall within reach. Squads cheer as they go over the top, and the enemy's counter-attack
    is heard from its front line. The gas alarm (a struck shell case) sounds when men nearby start masking, and the
    controlled soldier breathes through his mask.
- **Weather and the front** ([atmosphere-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/atmosphere-model.ts)):
  - Rain falls as hard as the weather says (snow is silent), and the wind gusts once it blows harder than a breeze.
  - Thunder follows each lightning flash at about 3 s a kilometre.
  - As the camera climbs, the detail gives way to the whole front's guns from far off, louder the heavier the recent
    fighting.
- **The level's own sounds** ([level-sound-model.ts](../apps/client/src/audio/level-sound-model.ts)): braziers and
  burning wrecks crackle as the camera nears them, crows caw as a flock lifts off the wire, and Very lights go up with a
  pop and a fizz.

The sounds come from Pole Position's Warfare Library, BOOM's World War I Firearms, Super Thump's Weapons of World War II
and the Sonniss GDC bundles. The incoming rush is the run-up of two designed Bluezone sounds (no designed explosion is
used); the ringing and the reverbs are generated. `scripts/build-sound-effects.py` cuts them:

- It finds each take in the long source recordings and cuts it until it dies away.
- It loops beds with a crossfade, and cuts the loudest stretches of recordings that never fall quiet.
- It matches each sound's variations in loudness, with a limiter catching the peaks.

```
blender -b --factory-startup -P scripts/build-sound-effects.py -- vendor/audio .asset-cache/level
```

It writes mono Opus files and a manifest to `.asset-cache/level/sfx/`, about 4 MB, swapping them in file by file. The
asset pack encrypts them with the rest of the kit. The ambience beds come from the kit build's `audio` section. What's
still missing (recordings, game hooks and sound design) is listed in [sound](sound.md).

## Level kit

`scripts/build-level-kit.py` builds the kit from the converted Synty packs (see the
[asset pipeline](assets/pipeline.md)):

```
blender -b --factory-startup -P scripts/build-level-kit.py -- .asset-cache/unity vendor/unity .asset-cache/level
```

It writes `.asset-cache/level/kit.glb`: about 100 pieces from War Map WWI, War Pack, Nature and the generic props,
joined one mesh per piece, sharing 1K palettes, with a decimated far-distance copy of every piece over 700 triangles. It
also writes four ground textures from the Nature pack with their normal maps, graded so their mean colours match the
terrain's flat palette colours; effect sprites from the Particle FX pack (smoke, mist, a flipbook of flames, a spark,
and a faceted dark blot for scorch and flying earth); and the ambience, cut and encoded with Blender's audio module.

The dev server serves the raw kit at `/level-kit/`. **Production builds never ship it raw**: paid assets ship only
inside the encrypted asset pack below ([licensing](assets/licensing.md)). Without the kit the game falls back to the
greybox: flat-coloured terrain with plain carved trenches.

In the game, placements are drawn in batches ([scenery-batches.ts](../apps/client/src/level/scenery-batches.ts)): every
placement of a kit piece within a 128 m cell of the map is a thin instance of one mesh, or within the whole map for a
piece placed fewer than about fifty times. Each batch has a Geometry of its own over the piece's vertex and index
buffers, which aren't copied: Babylon keeps thin instances in the Geometry, so batches sharing one would draw each
other's. The sector's 14,500 props make about 525 batches, so Babylon, which tests every mesh against the view every
frame (again for each shadow cascade and for the depth of field's depth pass), tests a few hundred meshes instead of
14,500. Heavy pieces switch to their far copy beyond 110 m and small clutter such as duckboards and splinters stops
drawing beyond 50–220 m, both measured to the nearest point of the batch, so pieces near the camera always draw. Grass,
litter, planks and the like cast no shadows and stay out of the depth of field's depth map, which is drawn at half the
screen's size. Zoomed fully out the sector draws about 1.6 M triangles.

### Asset pack

`pnpm assets:pack` (`scripts/build-asset-pack.mjs`) packs every file under `.asset-cache/level/`, keyed by its path
relative to it (`kit.glb`, `ground/mud.png`, `fx/smoke.png`), into `.asset-cache/pack/level.pack`, and writes the key to
`.asset-cache/pack/level.key.json`. Rerun it after every kit build. Both files are git-ignored; never commit them.

The pack is a custom container: a 32-byte header (magic `8E "VDNPK" 0D 0A`, format version, index length and IV), an
encrypted index of `{ path, offset, length, iv }` entries, then the files. Everything after the header is AES-256-GCM
under a key drawn fresh on every run. Each file has its own IV and is bound to its path; the index is bound to the
header. Tampering with any byte makes decryption fail, and nothing in the pack is readable without the key.

`pnpm -F @verdun/client build` picks the pack up if it exists: it emits it as `assets/<content hash>.bin` and bakes its
URL and the base64 key into the bundle as `import.meta.env.VERDUN_LEVEL_PACK_URL` and `_KEY` (the `assetPack` plugin in
`apps/client/vite.config.ts`). In the browser, `src/level/kit-files.ts` starts a module worker
(`src/assets/pack-worker.ts`) that fetches the pack once, decrypts the index with WebCrypto, and decrypts each file on
request, handing it to the page without a copy. The console logs `Level kit: opened the asset pack` when it opens. With
no pack the build still succeeds, as in CI, and the game stays greybox.

The key ships in the bundle, so the pack stops casual extraction, not a determined one. Whether that counts as
"reasonable measures" is on the licensing doc's legal-review list; signed, short-lived pack URLs come with a server.

In dev, `?pack` reads the pack through the worker instead of the raw `/level-kit/` route: the dev server serves it under
`/level-pack/`. To try a production build, run `pnpm assets:pack`, then `pnpm -F @verdun/client build` and
`pnpm -F @verdun/client preview` (the `client-preview` launch configuration).

**Open problem:** CI builds from git, which has no vendor assets, so its builds are greybox. A deployed build needs the
pack delivered to the build somehow, such as a private artifact store the deploy job downloads from, or building and
deploying from a machine that holds the vendor files. Not decided yet.

## Editing in Tiled

The layout (trench lines, wire belts and scenery) can be edited in [Tiled](https://www.mapeditor.org), as the
[editor plan](tooling.md#editor-plan) proposes. [tiled.ts](../packages/map/src/tiled.ts) converts between our map format
and a Tiled JSON map (`.tmj`). Heights, surfaces and craters stay generated.

```
pnpm map:export-tiled                                 # writes maps/fleury.tmj from generateFleurySector()
node scripts/export-tiled.mjs --check maps/fleury.tmj # reads an edited map and lists kit warnings
```

**Conventions.** An orthogonal map with no tilesets and one-metre tiles at 10 px per metre, so the grid and snapping
step is 1 m and Tiled's tile coordinates read as metres. Tiled's x is our x × 10. Tiled's y grows downward and our z
grows north, so y is flipped about the bottom edge: y = (depth − z) × 10, with depth the map's height in metres. North
is up on screen. The exporter rounds positions to the millimetre, headings to 0.01° and scales to 0.0001, and writes
keys in Tiled's alphabetical order with one object per line, so a moved tree is a one-line diff. Once Tiled re-saves the
file it uses its own layout.

| Layer      | Objects   | Data                                                                                         |
| ---------- | --------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `trenches` | Polylines | String properties `kind` (`fire` or `communication`) and `facing` (`north`, `south`, `none`) |
| `wire`     | Polylines | One belt each                                                                                |
| `scenery`  | Points    | Class (Tiled's object type) is the kind; float `heading`, float `scale`, int `variant`       |

Headings are in **degrees** clockwise from north, which is clockwise on screen (the map format stores radians). A
scenery point without properties gets heading 0, scale 1 and variant 0. Communication trenches may leave `facing` out.
Copying an existing object is the quickest way to get its properties. A rotated polyline is read with its rotation
applied. Other layers, such as a reference image a designer adds, are ignored.

**Validation.** `fromTiledMap` throws a `TypeError` naming the Tiled object and layer when something can't be read: a
missing layer, a rectangle where a polyline belongs, an unknown kind or class, a fire trench without a facing, a
negative scale. Places the trench kit can't line are warnings instead, listed by object:

- turns that aren't right angles;
- less than `TRENCH_GRID.clearance` (4.1 m) of straight trench between a free end and a turn, or twice that between two
  turns or junctions;
- a trench ending on another at an angle (a junction must be square: a T, or a corner where it meets another's end).

A trench whose end lies within 5 cm of another trench's centre line joins it there. The generated Fleury sector has no
warnings, and a test keeps it that way.

**Using an edited layout.** `generateFleurySector({ layout: fromTiledMap(json) })` builds the sector around a Tiled
layout: its trenches, wire and scenery replace the generated ones, while heights, surfaces, roads and craters stay
seeded. Pools and bridges follow the craters and roads, so they are always regenerated. Each side's front line must
still span the map from west to east. Client and simulation must load the same layout.

## Dev tools

- `?webgl` forces WebGL2 in dev builds, to test the GLSL shaders. It also helps automated checks when the browser pane
  is hidden: a hidden page gets no animation frames, but under WebGL `view.scene.render()` followed by the canvas's
  `toDataURL()` still captures a frame.
- `window.verdun.level` holds the batched scenery (`scenery.batches`, `scenery.near(x, z, radius)`), the placements, any
  pieces the kit was missing, the atmosphere, the shell damage and the ambience.
- **Death map**: J toggles a faint red disc wherever a soldier has fallen. Discs pile into solid red on the killing
  grounds. The record survives reloads, so it builds up over a playtest; `window.verdun.deaths.clear()` starts afresh
  and `points()` exports it.
- `node scripts/analyze-sector.mjs`: dead ground and exposure, as above.
- `node scripts/fetch-elevation.mjs`: downloads the surveyed ground again.
